The first level: a landslide across the route

2026-10-25

The first thing to play, at ?mode=level (the Play: landslide button). The mech starts at the home base on the gravel lot, walks east along the marker posts, and finds a narrow pass under the hill filled by a slide. Clearing the route's lane through the pass opens the way: Leave the base → Reach the landslide → Clear the rubble → Route open, with the objective at the top of the page and a chime at each step.

From the cockpit (first person, the default), arriving at the pass. The rubble is 48 chipped rocks, each a convex body in the mech's own Rapier world.

How the rubble works, and why this way

The simplest mechanic that keeps the physics honest: the rocks are dynamic convex hulls in the same Rapier world as the mech (mw_sim::rubble), so the feet and hull push them for real, and the heap (three layers, about 1.5 m) trips a mech that just walks in. The pass's sides and the play area's edges are fixed boxes under the hills. The mech's blade (E or Space, or the page's Shove button) gives every rock in a box ahead of the torso a push along the heading, with a little lift and spread; heavier rocks move less. Held down while walking, it ploughs. The route counts as open when no rock's centre is left in its lane through the pass: rock shoved against the cliffs is out of the way. Training never adds rocks or walls, so it steps exactly as before. Swapping the mechanic later means replacing Level::before_step (the shove) and Level::blocked (the test).

Third person (V), mid-clear: the lane is down to five rocks after one pass with the blade.

After a fall the mech stands up again at the last place it stood upright with no rock within 3 m, facing the way it faced there. (Standing it up where it fell, inside the heap, threw it over the pass's wall in a first try.)

Playing it

KeysW/S walk (Shift faster), A/D turn: the policy's walk command while held. E or Space: the blade. V: first or third person. R: start over. P: pause.

Measured: the shipped policy (teacher-v3), steered as a player would (examples/rubble_walk.rs, natively), opens the route in 96 s of simulated time with 156 shoves and no falls; in headless Chrome, driven with the keyboard, the whole level from the base took 110 s at 2× time.

Route open: the lane clear, the rocks against the pass's sides and beyond it.

Sound: why there was none

Neither the music nor the effects had ever played in a browser. It was not the autoplay policy: after the first click the AudioContext was running, but both AudioWorklet processors were created without processorOptions (the page posted raw wasm bytes over the port, which the processors never read), so the output stayed at exactly zero. The score's station table also lacked its required seed, and the music was off by default. Now the page compiles each wasm and hands the module over as the processors expect, tunes the music engine's orchestral station on its station parameter, starts both on the first click, tap or key (with a note on the page until then), and remembers if the viewer turns the music off. __mech.audioState() reports the context, both players and the output level: 0.010 RMS after a click in the viewer, 0.003 with the music off.

New effects in mw_audio: a stone knock for each rock that hits something (sized by its impulse, panned and attenuated from the cockpit), a grinding gravel bed for rock sliding and rolling, a two-note chime for an objective, and the blade's strike on rock as a hull ring. Footfalls and the wind were already there; the level runs a light desert wind.

Not done yet

Nobody has listened to the new sounds: they are checked by tests and levels only. In headless Chrome every mode renders much darker than the pictures further down this log (the public site's build too), so the pictures here are dark; whether a real browser shows the same is not checked. The hills off the pass are scenery: the play area's edges are invisible walls at their feet.

The estimator

The second half of Rapid Motor Adaptation. A per-step embedding (61 → 32) and three 1D convolutions over the last 50 policy steps (one second), then two heads: the teacher's body latent z (16, the target) and the 32 body parameters (an auxiliary guess, for the demo's bars). It trains online against the frozen teacher. For the first 2M steps the teacher drives on the true z; over the next 8M the envs hand over to the actor driving on the estimated z (DAgger), so the estimator learns from the states its own errors lead to. In Rust it keeps a ring of embedded steps and runs the convolutions each policy step: about 0.2 M multiply-adds.

The latent error falls to a third of z's variance, then rises a little as the estimator takes over driving (its own states are harder). It reads torso mass and control latency best, then centre of mass, leg lengths and drivers. Friction is nearly unreadable: on flat ground at these speeds the feet hardly ever slip, so nothing in the motion depends on it. It stopped improving by 10M steps, and the run was stopped at 17M to free the CPUs for teacher-v2.

Smooth movement: teacher-v2

The policy commands a new joint target at 50 Hz, and stiff ankle PD loops turn a target that flips each step into the foot wiggle the owner saw. The curriculum now steps on the fall rate over ten updates' episodes, not one update's handful. In its first 10M steps the joint-acceleration penalty fell from 2.0 to 0.78 per second, and action rate and jerk fell about 45%.

How it fits together

The policy never crosses an engine gap: training and the browser step the same Rust simulation.

The mech

An original biped of about 4 t and 3.2 m: a torso box and two legs of six revolute joints each (hip yaw, roll and pitch, knee, ankle pitch and roll), one Rapier multibody. Every joint is a PD position controller inside a motor-and-driver model: the commanded torque becomes a current, the driver clamps it (damage lowers the clamp), the winding heats with I²R and cools to ambient, the driver derates above 90 °C and trips at 120 °C, and a weak driver faults on a sustained overload. The policy sees the motor temperatures, as a real robot's would.

Finding 1: a standing mech's feet slid

The first stand test drifted backwards and tipped. The feet were sliding about 19 cm under a 20 kN load while their bodies reported zero velocity. Friction, solver iterations and contact stiffness made no difference. Rapier's position-stabilization pass corrects ground penetration without friction, and through the mech's bent legs that correction moves the feet sideways. With it off (num_internal_stabilization_iterations = 0) the slide is 2 mm and penetration stays under 2 mm.

Two more Rapier lessons from the same day: a multibody link's reported velocity is not its real velocity (so foot slip is measured from positions), and impulses applied to a link's rigid body are lost (so a push is a force held for one physics step). The PD control uses Rapier's implicit force-based joint motor, whose torque limit the actuator model sets each step.

Finding 2: the thermal trip could never fire

With the driver derating to 30% just below the trip temperature, a motor at full demand settles under the trip point (90 W of heat against 375 W of cooling). The floor is now 70%: an overload still trips, so a policy has to lighten a hot joint's load to keep it alive, which is the limp the spec wants to emerge.

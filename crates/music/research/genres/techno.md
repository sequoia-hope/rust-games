# Techno: research notes for the generator

Scope: Detroit techno, Berlin techno (Tresor, Basic Channel / dub techno, Berghain / Ostgut Ton), minimal (Hood, Plastikman, 2000s Kompakt/Perlon), acid house and acid techno (the TB-303 lineage), hard / industrial techno (Birmingham, Schranz, the 2020s fast scene) and melodic techno (Afterlife). Every claim carries a reference number; anything I could not verify is marked "(unverified)" or "(inference)". Step numbers use a 16-step bar, steps 1-16, so the four beats fall on steps 1, 5, 9 and 13.

A note on sources. Attack Magazine's "Beat Dissected" series is the best published source for techno drum programming. Its text gives tempo, swing, sounds and the role of each part, but the exact grids appear only as screenshots, which I could not read. Grid positions given below are therefore of three kinds: (a) positions the sources state outright (four-to-the-floor, off-beat open hat, snare on 2 and 4, Butler's 16-pulse transcription); (b) positions that follow directly from a stated description (for example, "on every second off-beat" means steps 7 and 15); and (c) conventions I could not find stated anywhere, which are marked (unverified).

## 3. Drums

### 3.1 The core grid, with what each part is supported by

| Part | Steps | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Kick | 1, 5, 9, 13 every bar ("four-to-the-floor with no variation" in the Detroit recreation) | [11][12][13][15] |
| Open hat | 3, 7, 11, 15 (on every off-beat) | [11][15] |
| Snare and/or clap | 5, 13 (beats 2 and 4) | [13][22][44]. The dub techno recreation puts the snare on "every other downbeat" and doubles it with a pitched-up clap [15] |
| Closed hat, 16ths | all 16 steps, the 4th 16th of each beat nudged late, light random velocity | [17] |
| Shaker ("hat 2") | 7, 15 (on every second off-beat) | [13] |
| Ride | 8th notes (odd steps), every second hit shorter and quieter, so the off-beats are quieter; low end cut below 1 kHz | [12] |
| Rimshot | anywhere except the kick steps: it "never lands on the same beat as a kick" | [11] |
| Detroit hats | two closed hats (one sharp, one soft with a slightly longer decay) plus the off-beat open hat; putting the closed hats in the open hat's choke group gives a tight sound, separate groups a loose one | [11] |
| Ghost kicks | two extra kicks in bar 2 of a 2-bar loop, each nudged off the grid by a slightly different amount | [14] |
| Perc growl | one hit on an off-beat near the start of each bar (step 3 by the description) | [14] |
| Pre-beat wobble | a short LFO "glitch" just before beat 2 (step 4 or 5) | [14] |

### 3.2 Pattern families beyond the backbeat

- **3+3+3+3+4 against the kick.** Butler transcribes 808 State's "Cubik" (1990) as a single-pitch synth on pulses 1, 4, 7, 10 and 13 of 16, over even quarter-note kicks [25]. This 16-pulse "almost-even" grouping is the cleanest documented cross-rhythm in this music. Butler also finds 3+3+2 on an 8-pulse grid and displacement dissonance: parts shifted by one 16th or one 8th against the kick ("Piku", "Cups") [25].
- **Polymeter / phasing.** On *Consumed*, Hawtin set irregular step cycles of five and twelve notes to move filters over time, so the parts cross-modulate [27]. For acid, MusicTech suggests cutting a 16-step 303 pattern to 15 or 13 steps so it cycles against the bar ("woozy and unstable") [9].
- **Rolls in 32nds.** The "Spastik" recreation writes its 808 snare roll on a 1/32 grid. The sixth 32nd of a beat is delayed in two places, and bar 3 adds one extra 32nd right after the downbeat [17]. The original is a 9-minute track built on TR-808 percussion [47].
- **Detroit "Strings of Life".** The track has no bassline. The kick and a shaker enter only in section 6. A 909 snare on 2 and 4 plays with rolling 16th hats for two bars; a ride plays four to the bar on the kick beats; TR-727 bongos come in later. "The kick is frequently dropped or broken up" [18].
- **Robert Hood's minimal.** Motifs often last a single bar, and the percussion can be just kick and hi-hat. It is "forged not through addition, but subtraction" [29]. Hood: "Take your time, and get into a groove… and stick to it" [29].
- **Industrial.** The kick is a 909 with 250-300 Hz cut out "making the sub emerge", then bitcrushed. A bitcrushed 909 low tom fills the low-mids the kick leaves open, with its velocity lowered wherever it coincides with the kick or hat. A 6-bit woodblock is layered on the toms. The snare is noise-based, on beats 2 and 4 [13].

### 3.3 Kick and rumble sound

- 909: released in 1983, about 10,000 units built. The kick, snare and toms are analogue; the hats, ride and crash are 6-bit samples. The kick has attack and decay controls; the sequencer has shuffle and flam [10].
- The "dark techno rumble" recipe: a compressed 909-style kick with a strong pitch envelope, short decay, everything below about 50 Hz cut, and -3 dB around 500 Hz. Under it, a second kick with a long decay and a softened attack, sent through a long, wet reverb, then a low-pass filter with raised resonance and drive just under 4 dB, ducked so the main kick has room. The drum bus gets Drive 17% and Boom 45% at 55 Hz [12].
- In Detroit: 909 samples run through an MPC60 for "12-bit crunch", light distortion, and little velocity variation; the movement comes from how the parts interact [11].

## 4. Bass and the TB-303

### 4.1 Bass by substyle
- **Detroit:** can have no bass at all ("Strings of Life") [18]; the groove comes from drums, strings and piano.
- **Dub techno:** a short sub-bass sample, pitched to the root (D#) and the fourth (G#), with a short release and ducked by the kick [15]. Wikipedia describes "chorded layers of elaborate basslines" [31].
- **Minimal:** the energy sits in the middle frequencies more than in deep bass [30].
- **Melodic techno:** a Moog-style bass on the chord roots in 1/8 and 1/16 pulses, sidechained [44]; "deep, continuous basslines" [43].
- **Dark / Berghain:** the rumble kick does the bass's job (§3.3) [12]. Its link to Berghain is my inference, though RA's "throbbing… rigid beat and determined bassline" wording fits it [35].
- **Acid house and acid techno:** a 303 line (below).

### 4.2 TB-303 facts
- Released in 1981 (some sources say 1982), discontinued in 1984. Saw or square wave. Wikipedia calls the filter a 24 dB/oct resonant low-pass that does not self-oscillate [6][8].
- The sequencer plays 16th notes. A normal note is gated for 7 of 12 clock ticks (about 58% of the step); a slid note is held for all 12 ticks into the next note [8], citing Robin Whittle.
- **Two envelopes** (Robin Whittle, the Devil Fish designer) [7]:
  - The VCA envelope has a sharp attack and a fixed, fairly long exponential decay.
  - The main envelope drives the filter. Its decay comes from the Decay knob on normal notes, but is forced to the knob's minimum on accented notes. On a stock unit, Env Mod cannot be turned down to zero.
- **Accent** [7]:
  - On accented notes, the main envelope is also added to the VCA through a 47 kΩ / 0.033 µF RC network, which softens the attack. This is why accents are louder.
  - It also goes through the "Accent Sweep Circuit" (diode, 47k, 100k pot, 1 µF capacitor) to raise the cutoff.
  - The sweep shares a gang with the Resonance pot, so it grows stronger as resonance rises.
  - The capacitor does not fully discharge between notes, so a run of consecutive accents peaks higher each time. Whittle links that rising curve to the acid sound.
- **Glide time:** I could not find a primary hardware figure. The Open303-based NT-303 emulation defaults to 60 ms of slide and 300 ms of filter decay [49] (an emulation default, not a measurement).

### 4.3 How acid lines are played and programmed
- "Acid Tracks" came out of a jam: Spanky had a beat going on a TR-707 and TR-727, and Pierre "got on the knobs" of the 303, changing cutoff and resonance; "we were there for over an hour just playing around" [5]. The track's motion is the knob moves, not a written melody.
- The squelch comes from high resonance and low cutoff, with the accent, slide and octave settings varied over a simple pattern [3][39].
- Programming method: enter 16 notes quickly, place accents and slides at random, keep octave jumps within +1 octave (as on the hardware), and shorten the pattern to 13 or 15 steps against the drums. Distortion comes after the 303 [9].

## 5. Harmony

- "Conventional harmonic practice is often set aside in favour of repetitive note sequences"; vocals and melody are uncommon [2]. Minimal techno focuses on "rhythm and repetition instead of melody and linear progression" [30].
- **Mode:** Attack: "99.9% of techno is minor". Its pad tutorial builds triads, 7ths and 9ths from natural minor (E minor). It alternates Bm and Em, four bars each, and puts G major (III) into bars 3 and 7 of the 8-bar cycle: in E minor that is v–i with III as a passing colour [19]. I found no source for Phrygian (♭2) as a techno habit, so Phrygian is (unverified).
- **Dub techno:** one minor chord (D# minor in the recreation) chopped into syncopated stabs, plus the same chord sustained underneath; the sub bass moves between root and fourth [15]. Chords "tend to sound hollow", so scoop the mids [16].
- **Detroit:** "Strings of Life" is four piano chords, with orchestral string stabs hitting "on the first beat of each phrase" and quieter sustained notes between hits. Later the synth rhythm shifts against the unchanged string pattern to create tension [18]. On Detroit-style chords, practitioner forums point to minor 7th chords played as one-finger "chord memory" shapes moved in parallel (Juno-106, DX100) (forum evidence only, unverified).
- **Melodic techno:** minor keys or modes [43]. A tutorial gives i–VI–III–VII (Am–F–C–G) or i–iv–v–III (Am–Dm–Em–C) in A minor, 2-3 pad layers, and filter automation over 16 bars [44].
- **Acid:** single-key 303 riffs. MusicTech advises entering random pitches inside a chosen key [9]; I found no published scale rule for 303 riffs.

## 7. Arrangement and energy

### 7.1 General
- The loop is "the fundamental unit of musical structure for EDM" (Butler, quoted by Garcia). Techno emphasises "process more than the construction of particular musical objects" [26].
- **Accumulative form:** a groove is built up "gradually from its constituent parts" (Spicer, quoted by Garcia). Layering, adding and subtracting loops gives "an ever-changing same" [26].
- Structural changes are expected on multiples of 4 (4 beats, 4 bars, 8 bars or more) [26]. Attack: dance music is arranged in 8- or 16-bar blocks; start with the kick and add one element every 8 or 16 bars. The layout runs intro, breakdown 1, drop 1, breakdown 2, drop 2, cool-down, outro, and the ending removes elements in reverse order [20].
- **Forecasting by filter and subtraction.** In Plastikman's "ethnik" (1994), a 4-bar flute line fades in over an ambiguous bassline. A three-part conga loop then enters suddenly, marking a structural downbeat. An EQ filter shift on the drum loop forecasts the peak; the triangle loop drops out 4 bars before the peak and the drum loop 1 bar before [26].
- **Track length:** "Acid Tracks" runs 12:15 [4] and "Spastik" 9:22 [47]. On "Spastik", Hawtin "unleashes the monstrous bass drum kicks after several minutes of slow buildup" [47]. The Attack recreation compresses that into 4 bars: the kick is high-passed at 82 Hz for bars 1-2 and opened at the downbeat of bar 3 [17].
- **Long single-take processes:** "Consumed" ran an 8- or 16-bar Atari loop overnight while mixer mute automation switched effects in and out, and it was recorded in one take. Edits were limited to trimming starts, shortening middles and cutting ends. Hawtin spoke of "the beauty of having an hour-long listening experience" [27].
- **Filter motion as the change:** cutoff automation creates the variation over time [48]. One pad recipe opens the cutoff from 50 Hz up to high across each 4-bar section [19]. In melodic techno the cutoff moves over 16 bars [44]. FX layers last 16-32 bars, and an intro is made by stripping elements out of the main section [48].

### 7.2 Substyle templates
- **Detroit ("Strings of Life"):** solo piano intro; the piano chords jump up an octave to anticipate the drums; drums and shaker enter in section 6; snare-roll fills in sections 7, 11 and 15; new piano and string parts in section 13; a return to the riff; abrupt volume cuts and a fade [18].
- **Berghain DJ ethic (Klock):** "hypnotic grooves and gradual builds"; the mix lacks individually explosive moments; "the subtle progression is what makes the pay-off so rewarding" [35]. The kick stays steady and there are no big breakdowns (search-result summary of reviews; RA does not state this outright) [35].
- **Melodic techno:** intro (bars 1-16: kick, hats, atmosphere), build (17-32: add bass, FX, pads), drop (33-48: full drums, lead, bass), breakdown (49-64: pads and FX only), climax (65-80: everything plus more automation). The published bar numbers overlap by one bar, so I normalised them to 16-bar blocks [44].
- **Birmingham:** live stereo takes, then 6-9 tracks layered and edited [28]. Regis: "wave upon wave of drums, percussive clutter and background grit", often without melody; abrupt endings (a blown monitor meant the last three minutes of some records were mixed blind) [41].

## Rules and parameters

### Tempo & feel
- Pick BPM by substyle: Detroit 120-125; acid house 118-122 ("Acid Tracks" ≈120 as released); minimal 125-130; dub techno 112-125 (allow a 140-145 variant at low weight); rumble / Berghain 128-135; industrial 126-130; grinding analogue 125-132; hard techno 140-160; melodic 120-128. [2][4][11][12][13][14][15][24][30][31][43]
- Swing as a Linn-style delay of the even 16ths: Detroit 55-60%; rumble 50-54%; industrial 50-60%; grinding 50-65%; dub techno and hard techno 50%; melodic 50-54%. Default 52-54% to loosen straight parts. [11][12][13][14][15][21]
- Hold one tempo for the whole track; no tempo changes inside a track. [27][35] (inference from long single-take practice)

### Drums
- Kick on steps 1, 5, 9, 13 in every bar where drums play; no kick variation in Detroit mode. [11][12][13][15]
- Open hat on steps 3, 7, 11, 15. [11][15]
- Snare or clap on steps 5 and 13; in dub mode, put the snare on steps 5 and 13 and double it with a clap pitched up 3-7 semitones (exact pitch unverified). [13][15][22]
- Closed hats on all 16 steps, velocity random ±10%, every 4th step (4, 8, 12, 16) delayed by about 1/64 of a bar. [17]
- Shaker on steps 7 and 15; or on every off-beat with alternate hits 20-30% quieter. [12][13]
- Ride on odd steps (8ths); hits on 3, 7, 11, 15 shorter and 6 dB quieter; high-pass the ride at 1 kHz. [12]
- Rimshot / perc line: never on steps 1, 5, 9, 13. [11]
- Put two ghost kicks in bar 2 of a 2-bar loop at 25-40% velocity, each 2-8 ms off the grid (the offsets are unverified). [14]
- Butler cross-rhythm: one single-pitch voice on steps 1, 4, 7, 10, 13 (3+3+3+3+4); allow a one-16th or one-8th displaced copy of a part as metrical dissonance. [25]
- Polymeter: give 1-2 voices (perc, 303, or a filter LFO) a cycle of 3, 5, 12, 13 or 15 steps against the 16-step bar; never more than 2 at once (the cap is unverified). [9][25][27]
- Allow 808 snare rolls on a 32nd grid (needs 32nd support), with one 32nd per beat delayed; use them in "Spastik" / percussive minimal mode. [17]
- Detroit percussion set: 909 kit through a 12-bit crush; two closed hats (sharp and soft) and a choke group on the open hat, toggled per section. [11]
- Industrial mode: 909 low-tom line on the gaps between kicks; drop its velocity 30-50% whenever it coincides with a kick or hat. [13]

### Bass
- Rumble / Berghain mode: no separate bass. Derive a rumble from the kick: long decay, soft attack, long (3-4 s) reverb at high wet, then a resonant low-pass and drive, ducked by the kick. [12]
- Dub mode: short sub notes on the root and the fourth only, short release, sidechained to the kick. [15]
- Melodic mode: Moog-style mono on the chord roots in 8th and 16th pulses, sidechained. [44]
- Detroit mode: allow a "no bass" variant (probability 0.2-0.3), with chords and drums carrying the low mids (the probability is mine, unverified). [18]
- Acid mode: one 303 line in 16ths. Gate normal notes at 7/12 of the step and tie slid notes for the full step; 25-40% of steps accented, 10-25% slid (the percentages are unverified); octave jumps of at most +1 octave. [8][9]
- Accented notes on the 303: force the filter-envelope decay to its minimum, add the filter envelope into the VCA with a softened attack, and add a cutoff sweep scaled by resonance. Let the sweep accumulate across consecutive accents (do not reset the sweep capacitor between notes). [7]
- 303 env mod floor greater than 0 (the stock unit cannot reach zero). [7]
- Glide 50-70 ms (emulation default 60 ms; the hardware value is unverified). [49]

### Harmony
- Default mode: natural minor. Major-key techno is under 5% probability ("99.9% of techno is minor"). [19]
- Most tracks stay on one chord or one riff: give 60-70% of tracks no chord change (the share is unverified; techno sets aside "conventional harmonic practice"). [2][15]
- Two-chord option: i and v (or i and iv), 4 bars each, with III as a passing chord in bars 3 and 7 of an 8-bar cycle. [19]
- Dub: one minor triad or minor 7th, chopped into syncopated stabs, plus a sustained copy underneath. [15][16]
- Detroit: a 4-chord minor loop (minor 7ths moved in parallel as a single "chord memory" shape is forum-sourced and unverified); string stabs on beat 1 of each phrase. [18]
- Melodic: i–VI–III–VII or i–iv–v–III, one chord per bar or per 2 bars. [44]
- Phrygian ♭2: allow as a low-weight (≤10%) colour only; no source found (unverified).

### Melody / hooks
- Default: no lead melody. Hooks are a riff of 1-4 notes, a stab, or a 303 line. [2][29][30]
- Motif length: 1 bar (Hood minimal) to 4 bars (Plastikman "ethnik" flute). [26][29]
- Melodic mode only: a minor-key arpeggio in 16ths, with delay and reverb doing much of the work. [43][44]
- Acid: generate the riff by random pitches inside the key, then fix it; vary only accent, slide and cutoff afterwards. [5][9]

### Sound design
- Kick: 909-style analogue kick with a strong pitch envelope; for hard and industrial modes add saturation, cut 250-300 Hz, then bitcrush. [12][13][14]
- Hats and cymbals: 6-bit quantised noise or metal for 909 authenticity. [10]
- Dub chord chain: saw + square + a little noise → bandpass ~450 Hz (Q low-moderate) with a slow LFO on cutoff → bit reduction → EQ -7 dB at 600 Hz, high-pass 100 Hz, low-pass 4.5 kHz → send delay: dotted 1/8 at 20% feedback plus a tape echo with 80-85% feedback, filtered on the send by a 0.25 Hz LFO → short reverb → tape hiss. [15][16]
- Detroit: sampled-strings-style pad (Mirage-ish, short attack) and a 12-bit crush on the whole kit. [11][18]
- Minimal: emphasise the mid band; keep subs sparse. [30]
- Acid techno: 303 into distortion with dynamics after it. [9][40]
- Add a "resonance crack" option: let the filter self-distort at the peak of a long sweep, and keep it. [27]

### Arrangement & energy
- Make every structural change on a 4-, 8- or 16-bar boundary; default to 16. [20][26]
- Additive intro: start with the kick (or the kick plus one perc), then add one element every 8 or 16 bars. [20]
- Outro: remove elements in the reverse order of entry, one per 8 or 16 bars. [20]
- Intro for DJ mixing: drums only for at least 16-32 bars (bar count unverified; the drums-first practice is sourced). [20][48]
- Main motion: one long cutoff sweep per section on the lead, chord or 303, lasting 16-32 bars; on pads, sweep up from 50 Hz across each 4-bar group. [19][44][48]
- Forecast the peak: filter-shift the drum loop 8-16 bars before it, remove a top layer 4 bars before and the drum loop 1 bar before, then bring everything back on the downbeat. [26]
- Breakdowns: Berghain mode has 0-1 breakdowns of at most 8 bars; Detroit has 1-3 kick dropouts of 1-4 bars; melodic has one 16-bar breakdown (pads and FX), then a 16-bar climax. [18][35][44]
- Track length: 6-10 minutes (≈192-320 bars at 128 BPM); an "extended" variant runs 12+ minutes for acid and minimal. [4][47]
- Element lifetime: a loop runs at least 16 bars unchanged; a new element or mute change comes every 8-16 bars; the filter moves continuously. [20][26][27]
- Melodic template: intro 16 → build 16 → drop 16 → breakdown 16 → climax 16 → outro 16-32. [44]
- Detroit template: solo chord / piano intro of 8-16 bars → chords jump up an octave as a pre-drum cue → kick and shaker enter → snare-roll fills at the ends of sections → a new chord-part section → riff returns → abrupt cuts and a fade. [18]

### Variation / surprise
- Kick dropout: remove the kick for 1-4 bars, or "break up" its pattern, 1-3 times per track (Detroit). [18]
- Kick high-pass reveal: high-pass the kick at ~80 Hz for 2-8 bars, then open it on a downbeat. [17]
- Off-grid ghost kick or a single delayed 32nd as a micro-surprise, once per 4-8 bars. [14][17]
- Pitch drop: a one-shot ride or perc hit at the end of the phrase whose pitch falls by 4 semitones right after the hit. [17]
- Choke-group toggle on the hats at a section change (tight ↔ loose). [11]
- Shift a stab's rhythm against an unchanged chord or string pattern for 8 bars to build tension. [18]
- Run one odd-length loop (13 or 15 steps) so phase alignment returns every 13 or 15 bars. [9]
- Effects gating: switch the reverb or delay send on and off on bar boundaries (mute automation). [27]
- Accent bursts on the 303: 3-5 consecutive accents, so the cumulative sweep climbs. [7]
- Dub echo throw: raise the feedback of the tape delay to ≥85% on one stab, then cut it after 1-2 bars (the throw technique is inference from [16]'s feedback settings).

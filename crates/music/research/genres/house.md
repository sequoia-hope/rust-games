# House music: Chicago, deep, garage, French/filter, UK garage and 2-step

Research notes for the procedural music engine's house family. Every claim is
cited to the numbered references at the end. Where a source gives a feel but no
number, the text says so. Rules the sources do not back, which are engine
design choices, are marked **(engine default)**. Step numbers are 1–16 within
one 4/4 bar of 16th notes: the beats are steps 1, 5, 9 and 13, and the
off-beat 8ths are 3, 7, 11 and 15.

## 2. Chicago house

### Origins and landmarks
- House grew out of Chicago's queer Black underground club culture in the early-to-mid 1980s. DJs reworked disco records to give them a more mechanical beat [1].
- Frankie Knuckles was resident DJ at the Warehouse (1977–82), then at the Power House. Ron Hardy played the Music Box [1]. Knuckles spliced records together when they were too short for his dancers [1].
- Accounts of where the name came from conflict: record-store bins marked "As Heard at the Warehouse", Larry Heard's "music made at home", Juan Atkins's "a club's house records" [1].
- Labels: Trax Records and DJ International had wider distribution and spread the sound [1].

| Year | Track | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| 1984 | Jesse Saunders, "On & On" | often cited as the first house record; TB-303, TR-808, Korg Poly-61 [1] |
| 1985 | Steve "Silk" Hurley, "Music Is the Key" | early house [1] |
| 1985 | Mr. Fingers (Larry Heard), "Mystery of Love" | early deep house [2] |
| 1986 | Mr. Fingers, "Can You Feel It" | Juno-60 and TR-909 only [16]; the emblem of deep house [2] |
| 1986 | Marshall Jefferson, "Move Your Body (The House Music Anthem)" | Trax [1]; piano and strings on a Prophet 2000, bass on a Roland JX-8P [42]; TR-707 [20] |
| 1986 | Steve "Silk" Hurley, "Jack Your Body" | UK no. 1 in January 1987 [1] |
| 1987 | Frankie Knuckles feat. Jamie Principle, "Your Love" / "Baby Wants to Ride" | Trax; Principle's original was "little more than vocals and TR-909 drums" [43] |
| 1987 | Phuture, "Acid Tracks" | Trax, produced by Marshall Jefferson; TB-303; 12:15 long; ~130 BPM slowed to ~120 at Jefferson's suggestion [18] |

### Drum machines
- The TR-808, TR-909 and TR-707 were all common [1].
- **TR-909** (1983, about 10,000 units): analogue kick, snare, toms, rimshot and clap; hats, crash and ride are 6-bit samples. The sequencer has shuffle and flam controls [19]. Knuckles bought second-hand 909s [19].
- **TR-707** (1985): Roland's first all-sample machine. Crash and ride are 6-bit, the rest 8-bit, and the sounds cannot be tuned. It has a tambourine, cowbell and rimshot, and was "a staple in early house music, particularly with acid house"; it was used on "Move Your Body" and "Washing Machine" [20].
- Chicago producers mostly recorded their rhythm tracks straight out of the hardware [6].

### The beat on the grid
Attack Magazine's 80s-style Chicago beat (TR-707 and TR-606 samples, 120–125 BPM, 50–55% swing) [6]:

| Voice | Steps (bar 1) | Bar 2 differences |
|---|---|---|
| Kick | 1, 5, 9, 13 + 15 (the last off-beat) | 1, 5, 9, 13 + a quiet ghost kick mid-bar (exact step not given) |
| Snare | 5, 13 | same |
| Clap (layered on snare) | 5, 13, 16 | same |
| Closed hat | all 16; every other hat (the even steps) quieter, with varied velocities | same |
| Open hat | 3, 7, 11, 15, at full velocity | same |
| 707 rimshot | even steps: 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16 | same |

Attack's "21st century Chicago house" (123 BPM, 55–60% swing): a 707 kick tuned down about a whole tone, four-on-the-floor, with an optional **lower-velocity turnaround kick on the final off-beat of a section**. It has 16th closed hats with varied velocities, a 909 open hat on some off-beats, choked by the closed hat, and a 909+707 clap layer. The 707 tambourine plays on every off-beat, doubled with one copy detuned and the two panned hard left and right. 707 rimshots go around the tambourine and open hat to push the syncopation [5]. The "bumpin' house" variant (122–127 BPM) puts a single ride at the start of a 2-bar loop, a low tom on the last off-beat of bar 1 (step 15), and mid toms a few 16ths before it, with a syncopated kick at the end of bar 2 [7].

Wikipedia's summary agrees: four-on-the-floor kick; snare or clap on 2 and 4; off-beat hats, claps, snaps and shakers for syncopation; early Chicago tracks often used a clave-based riff [1].

### Bass and synths
- The low register matters most; tracks are often built on bass-heavy loops [1].
- "Move Your Body": piano on a Prophet 2000, strings made by playing half the piano line through a Prophet 2000 string patch, bass on a JX-8P [42].
- Chicago house bass, u-he's recipe on a Juno-60 model: mono; saw off, pulse only, sub at 0; **filter cutoff fully closed, opened only by an envelope at maximum depth** (decay ~36%, sustain 0, release ~33%); envelope follows velocity and key at ~70%; chorus wet ~90% [26].
- Juno-106 (1984, MIDI, 128 patches, portamento in place of the 60's arpeggiator) [45], and Roland says it became vital to emerging house and techno [25].

### Arrangement
- Tracks are built from **repeated 8-bar sections** and build by adding layers and raising the level [1].
- DJ-oriented length: "Acid Tracks" runs over 12 minutes [18].

## 3. Deep house

### Origins and landmarks
- Chicago house fused with "the lush chords of 1980s jazz-funk" and soul, from 1985 [2].
- Pioneers: Larry Heard, Marshall Jefferson, Frankie Knuckles, Ron Hardy, Ron Trent. NY/NJ: Larry Levan, Tony Humphries, Timmy Regisford, Boyd Jarvis. Detroit: Chez Damier, Carl Craig, Theo Parrish, Moodymann. Later: Kerri Chandler and others [2].
- "Can You Feel It" (1986): a Juno-60 and TR-909 only, recorded "one take, one pass" to cassette with extra parts played by hand. It has a "hollowed-out bassline", a "smooth three-chord synth line" with "elegantly clustered" notes reflecting Heard's jazz training, and a "hypnotic thump" of kick [16]. The bass line has been recreated by clocking the Juno-60's arpeggiator from the 909 [17]. One 1992 interview credits a Jupiter-6 instead (unverified) [17].
- Masters At Work, "To Be In Love" (MAW Dub, 1997): its intro string loop is a reference for deep-house chords [15].

### Character
- Spacious percussion, ambient mixes, steady hypnotic repetition, and **no build-up-and-release emphasis**. Gentler and more organic than other house, close to jazz and soul [2].
- Soft keyboard pads, muted basslines, soulful vocals [2].

### Drums
Attack's "rolling deep house" beat (909 samples, 125 BPM, swing 60–70%) [8]:

| Voice | Steps |
|---|---|
| Kick | 1, 5, 9, 13 (tape-saturated) |
| Closed hat | 16th pattern, with a quieter hit on step 10 for shuffle |
| Shaker | shares the closed-hat role; may share a choke group with it |
| 909 tom | 3, 9, 11 ("reinforces the shuffle and jacking feel") |
| Afro perc (optional) | 2, 10, tuned against the tom |

Keep every hit short with no overlaps [8].

### Harmony
- **Minor 7 and major 7 are the base. 9ths are often added, and 11ths too** [15]. Minor 9 is "the one main chord" to learn for deep house [48][49]. The MusicRadar construction is Cm9 = C Eb G Bb D, with inner voices moved up an octave for a brighter voicing [48].
- Four-bar loops transcribed by Attack [15]:
  - Fmaj7 – Em7 (in C: IVmaj7 – iii7; in A minor: VImaj7 – v7)
  - Fm7 – Abmaj7 – Cm7 (MAW "To Be In Love" strings; in C minor: iv7 – VImaj7 – i7)
  - Am7 – Em7 – Dm7 (i7 – v7 – iv7). The A minor 7 is voiced with its 7th dropped an octave, keeping the voices close and making a descending top line E–D–C.
- Voice-leading advice: thin chords out but **keep the 7th of every chord** [15].
- Stab synthesis: a minor-chord stab in one patch is three oscillators at 0, +3 and +7 semitones [50]. A min7 adds +10, and a min9 adds +14.

## 4. Garage house (New York / New Jersey) and piano house

- Garage house came from the Paradise Garage (Larry Levan) and Club Zanzibar (Newark) in the early-to-mid 1980s. It has **more gospel-influenced piano riffs and female vocals** than other house, a soulful R&B-derived sound, and sits closer to disco [38]. Its UK popularity in the 1990s led to UK garage [38].
- **Organ bass.** Robin S, "Show Me Love" (original 1990; StoneBridge remix 1992–93): StoneBridge's first remix was rejected, so he dialled the Korg M1 to the next preset, an organ ("Organ 2"), and replayed his bass line on it. The grinding intro is a Yamaha DX100 driven into distortion [22][23].
- Syntorial's recipe for that bass: mono; **triangle at 40% plus a pulse at +31 semitones at 60%**; low-pass at 50%; filter env depth 10% with decay 1800 ms and sustain 0; amp env A 0, D 1900 ms, S 0, R 200 ms; 1/8 delay at 25% wet with 20% feedback [24]. (Derived, not in the source: +31 semitones is within 0.02 semitone of the 6th harmonic, so an additive or FM equivalent is fundamental plus a strong 6th partial with a plucked decay.)
- **The M1 piano.** The Korg M1 (1988) uses 16-bit PCM ROM through a non-resonant digital filter [21]. Its "Piano 16'" and "Organ 2" presets were, per Sound On Sound, "audible on every house track for well over a decade"; Piano 16' "launched a thousand Italian piano house records" and Madonna's "Vogue" [21][46]. Italo house is built on "electronic piano chords" used more lyrically than in Chicago house; Black Box, "Ride on Time" (1989) is the best-known example [41].
- Gospel moves: the sources establish gospel-derived piano and vocals [38] but give no transcribed progression. The progression rules in §9 are marked **(engine default)**.

## 5. French house / filter house

### Origins and landmarks
- A French take on house from the early-to-mid 1990s, a form of Eurodisco. Thomas Bangalter's releases on his **Roulé** label were among the earliest. Influences: US dance music, Euro and space disco, P-Funk, and Chicago "jacking" [3]. "French touch" was popularised by Martin James in *Melody Maker* in 1996 [3].
- Daft Punk: "Da Funk" (Soma, 1995), "Indo Silver Club" (1996), *Homework* (January 1997, made at home in Paris 1994–96), "Around the World" (1997), "Burnin'" (1997), "Revolution 909" (1998) [31]. *Homework*'s gear list includes Juno-106, MC-202, MKS-80, TB-303, Prophet-VS, TR-707, TR-808, TR-909 and the Alesis 3630 compressor [30].
- **Stardust, "Music Sounds Better With You" (1998).** Bangalter, Alan Braxe and Benjamin Diamond, on Roulé. It loops Chaka Khan's "Fate" (1981), sampled on an E-mu SP-1200, with Rhodes, TR-909, a Korg Trident bassline, an Ensoniq ASR-10, and an Alesis 3630 compressing the vocals and the final mix. UK no. 2 [27]. Vice calls it the breakthrough of the scene [32].
- Cassius (Zdar and Boombass): "Foxxy" (1996), "Cassius 1999" (UK no. 7, 1999), "Feeling for You" (1999) [33]. Also: Modjo, "Lady" (a "heavily filtered take" on the Stardust sound), Le Knight Club [32], Motorbass, *Pansoul* (1996) [3].

### Production signature
- **Filter and phaser** effects on and around samples. Samples come from **late-1970s/early-1980s European disco**. Steady four-on-the-floor at 110–130 BPM [3].
- Filter house as Vice defines it: a disco-record sample, a heavy kick underneath, shaped with EQ [32].
- **Pumping.** Daft Punk sidechained a Karen Young "Hot Shot" sample from the kick on "Indo Silver Club": the sample stays loud but ducks on every kick [28]. The Ableton recreation uses a near-limiting ratio and sets attack and release by ear. No numbers are published [29]. The Alesis 3630 was the main compressor on *Homework* and *Discovery* [28][30].
- How long filter sweeps run is not documented in an authoritative source. Community guides say a low-pass opens over 8–16 bars (unverified).

### Synthesising the "filtered disco sample" without samples
**(engine default, built from the cited ingredients):** make a 1- or 2-bar "disco loop" with the engine's own instruments: Rhodes or clean electric piano chords (the Stardust loop has Rhodes [27]), a syncopated octave bass, a muted funk guitar pluck, and a string or brass stab. Sum them to a bus, then run the bus through a resonant low-pass with a slow, automated cutoff, a phaser, and a sidechain compressor keyed by the kick [3][28]. This "loop bus" is the French-house lead.

## 6. UK garage, speed garage and 2-step

### Origins and landmarks
- London, early-to-mid 1990s: NY garage house plus jungle, ragga/dancehall and R&B, spread by pirate radio. Dubs and instrumentals were the norm, with vocals cut into short samples [4].
- **Speed garage.** In 1994 DJ EZ played Todd Edwards' "The Praise" at ~130 BPM instead of the US ~120 [4]. Its traits: sped-up house beats, rolling snares, reversed or warped basslines, sub-bass, ragga vocals, spin-backs and reversed drums [4]. Sped-up NY 4/4 beats meet breakbeats, with snares over the 2nd and 4th kicks, a sweeping bass, and gunshot and siren effects [36]. Tracks: Armand Van Helden's remix of CJ Bolland "Sugar Is Sweeter" (c. 1996), his remix of Sneaker Pimps "Spin Spin Sugar" (1997), 187 Lockdown "Gunman" (1997), Double 99 "RipGroove" (1997) [36].
- **2-step (1998–2001)** "removed the second and fourth kick drums in each bar and shifted the third", drawing on hip hop, Soul II Soul and dancehall [4]. Tracks: MJ Cole, "Sincere" (1998, made in his bedroom on a sampler and home computer [47]) and "Crazy Love" (2000); Shanks & Bigfoot, "Sweet Like Chocolate" (1999, the first UK garage no. 1); Artful Dodger feat. Craig David, "Re-Rewind" (1999, UK no. 2, produced by Mark Hill and Pete Devereux) [4][37]; Craig David, "Fill Me In" (no. 1, April 2000) [4].
- **Todd Edwards** (NJ): "Guide My Soul" (1993), "Saved My Life" (1994); co-wrote and sang "Face to Face" on Daft Punk's *Discovery* (2001) [34]. His "vocal reconstruction" chops, layers and rearranges vocal samples over swung four-on-the-floor beats; Reynolds called it "a melodic-percussive honeycomb of blissful hiccups" [34]. Edwards credits MK as the "main inspiration" and Enya's use of her own harmonies as instruments. He picks syllables that sound good over the piano riff and aims for chords "that conjure positive emotions" [35]. Garage vocals are chopped, time-stretched and pitch-shifted as rhythmic instruments, with single syllables reversed or re-pitched [4].

### Drums
Wikipedia: shuffled, syncopated hats, cymbals and snares, over either a 4/4 kick or the irregular 2-step pattern [4].

**Native Instruments 2-step template** (132 BPM) [13]:

| Voice | Steps |
|---|---|
| Kick | 1, 11 ("first beat and halfway through the third") |
| Snare | 5, 13 |
| Open hat | 3, 7, 15, plus swung hits just after steps 8 and 10 |
| Closed hat | 1, 9, plus swung hits just after steps 2, 4 and 6 |

**Attack "UK garage"** (124–132 BPM, MPC16 swing 68–69%; MPC, Drumtrax and LinnDrum sounds) [9]: kick on beat 1 and the "and" of beat 3 (steps 1 and 11), a **double hit opening bar 2**, and the first kick of each bar slightly louder. Kick pitched up 3 semitones and shortened. **Two rimshots replace the snare**: one on the backbeats, both on deliberately loose syncopated hits; rimshot 2 is pitched up 2 semitones and they trade phrases by velocity. A soft-attack closed hat **avoids a sharp off-beat** ("less house-like"), a cabasa plays the off-beats in place of an open hat, and a crash marks bar 1 of a section.

**Attack "rolling 2-step"** (122–128 BPM, swing 60–65%, 2-bar loop) [10]: a deep 808 kick on beat 1 of bar 1 with a 909/707 layer, and **no kick on beat 1 of bar 2**. Snare composite (DM5 + 808 pitched up) on beats 2 and 4. Hat on every off-beat, short decay between closed and open. Shaker and a 16th tambourine with heavy velocity variation give the "roll".

**The Producer School** (136 BPM; ~130 leans classic, >138 leans speed garage): moves kicks off the beats, adds **a fast double kick before the downbeat with the second quieter**, a clap and rim layer on 2 and 4 plus two syncopated rims, **clap/rim fills at the end of every second bar at lower velocity**, an open hat on every off-beat, and closed hats on syncopated 16ths [14].

### Bass, harmony, arrangement
- Speed garage: warped, sweeping, jungle- and reggae-influenced bass [36]. Sub-bass and reverse-warped lines [4].
- NI template: a 4-bar, 3-note bass line looped across 8 bars, with an opening low-pass on a "Dred"-style bass, in A minor [13].
- Arrangement in the NI template: elements added gradually up to **the drop at bar 25**, **the beat cut for one bar before the drop**, the vocal replacing the arp at the drop, and a **bassless outro from bar 73** [13].

## 7. Tech house and progressive house (briefly)

- **Tech house**: techno's "rugged basslines" and "steely beats" with progressive house's harmony and groove, more laid-back than progressive. Booming house kicks give way to shorter, darker, often distorted techno kicks, with smaller hats, noisier snares and acid 303 lines [39]. Juno-style tech-house bass from Roland: square at 16', optional sub, moderate cutoff, filter env A 0 / S 0 with medium decay and release, amp env sustain 100% with no velocity, and a 1/8-dotted delay [25].
- **Progressive house**: intensity comes from regularly adding and removing layers. **Phrases are 4, 8 or 16 bars, each often starting with a new melody or rhythm.** Later tracks build for up to ~4 minutes, then a breakdown, then a climax. No anthemic choruses or drum rolls [40].

## 8. Swing: the numbers

- MPC/Linn swing delays every second 16th in each 8th; **50% is straight; 54% loosens the feel without sounding swung; 66% is perfect triplet swing; 50–70% holds the useful range** [11].
- Conventions differ: FL Studio and Cubase 0% = Linn 50%, and FL 100% = Logic 66.6% [12]. Maschine's "15%" in Attack's bumpin'-house beat [7] is on another scale and cannot be converted reliably.
- Engine formula (derived from [11]): with swing *s* (0.50–0.75), every even step is delayed by **(2s − 1) × one 16th**. So 55% = 0.10 of a 16th, 60% = 0.20, 66.7% = 0.33 (the triplet position), and 69% = 0.38.
- Per-genre values from the sources: 80s Chicago 50–55% [6]; modern Chicago 55–60% [5]; deep house 60–70% [8]; rolling 2-step 60–65% [10]; UK garage 68–69% [9].

## Rules and parameters

### Tempo & feel
- Chicago house: tempo 118–126 BPM (core 120–125). [1][5][6][18]
- Deep house: 115–125 BPM, default 122. [2][8]
- French/filter house: 115–128 BPM. [3]
- Garage house (US): 118–124 BPM. [4]
- Speed garage: 128–134 BPM. [4][36]
- UK garage / 2-step: 128–138 BPM, default 132–134. [4][9][13][14]
- Tech house: 122–128 BPM. **(engine default; tempo unverified)** [39]
- Swing: delay even 16ths by (2s−1)×16th. [11]
- Swing per sub-genre: 80s Chicago s=0.50–0.55; modern Chicago 0.55–0.60; deep house 0.58–0.68; filter house 0.52–0.58 **(engine default)**; 2-step 0.60–0.69; UKG 4x4 0.62–0.69. [5][6][8][9][10]
- Never exceed s=0.70 in house modes. [11]

### Drums
- Kick: four-on-the-floor on steps 1, 5, 9, 13 for all house modes except 2-step. [1][3][8]
- Clap/snare on 5 and 13 in every mode, 2-step included. [1][6][10][13]
- Open hat on 3, 7, 11, 15. In UKG, replace it 50% of the time with a cabasa or shaker on the same steps. [6][9][10]
- Closed hat on all 16 steps. Even steps at 55–75% of the odd steps' velocity, ±10% random. [5][6]
- Chicago: rimshot on even steps (2, 4, …, 16) at low velocity; extra clap on step 16 with p=0.5. [6]
- Chicago: tambourine on off-beats 3, 7, 11, 15, two detuned copies panned L/R. [5]
- Chicago kit: 707 kick tuned −2 semitones (or 909 kick); 909 hats with a short open-hat decay choked by the closed hat; 909+707 clap layer. [5][19][20]
- Deep house: 909 tom on steps 3, 9, 11; optional perc on 2 and 10; extra-quiet closed hat on step 10. [8]
- 2-step kick: steps 1 and 11 in bar 1; bar 2 either repeats it or opens with a double kick (steps 1 and 2, the second quieter) or drops beat 1 entirely. [4][9][10][13][14]
- 2-step: closed hats on 1 and 9 plus swung 2, 4, 6; extra swung open hats on 8 and 10. [13]
- UKG snare: two rimshot voices (the second +2 semitones). Rim A on 5 and 13, both on 1–3 syncopated steps chosen per 2 bars; let velocity alternate between them. [9][14]
- UKG kick: pitch +3 semitones, short decay; first kick of each bar +10% velocity. [9]
- UKG roll: 16th tambourine or shaker at 30–80% velocity with random accents. [10]
- Speed garage: 4/4 kick; snare on 5 and 13; 1-bar snare rolls (16ths, rising velocity) before section changes. [4][36]
- Tech house: short, dark, lightly distorted kick; small hats; noisy snare. [39]

### Bass
- Chicago bass: mono pulse; cutoff at minimum, opened by a fast envelope at full depth (decay ~35% of range, sustain 0); 70% velocity tracking; chorus. [26]
- Deep house bass: Juno-style sub-octave line with a "hollow" timbre (pulse/square, low cutoff, short decay), playing an arpeggiated or repeating 1-bar figure synced to the drum clock. [16][17][26]
- Organ bass (garage house, Show Me Love style): additive/FM sine fundamental + 6th harmonic at about 1.5× its level (from 40%/60%), plucked amp env (A 0, D 1.8–1.9 s, S 0, R 0.2 s), gentle low-pass, 1/8 delay at 25% wet / 20% feedback. [22][24]
- Tech house bass: square 16', optional sub, filter env A0 D≈R medium S0, amp env sustained, 1/8-dotted delay. [25]
- Speed garage bass: sub plus a warped, sweeping mid layer (filter or pitch LFO, 1/4–1 bar); allow reverse-envelope "spin-up" notes. **(engine default from descriptions)** [4][36]
- 2-step bass: a 3-note line over 4 bars, looped to 8, with an opening low-pass. [13]
- Lower register carries the track: the bass is never muted for more than 8 bars outside breakdowns and outros. **(engine default)** [1][13]

### Harmony
- Default chord quality: min7, with a 9th (p=0.6) and an 11th (p=0.3); maj7 for the VI and IV chords. [15][48][49]
- Keep the 7th in every voicing; drop other notes before the 7th. [15]
- Close voice-leading: allow drop-octave 7ths so the top voice moves by step. [15]
- Progression pool, 4-bar loops: i7–v7–iv7; iv7–VImaj7–i7; VImaj7–v7 (= IVmaj7–iii7 relative major); i7 alone (1-chord vamp); i7–iv7 (2-chord vamp). [15]
- Deep house: max 3 chords per loop; clustered close voicings. [16]
- Chord stab construction: root, +3, +7, +10, +14 semitones for min9. [48][50]
- Garage house, gospel moves: allow ii7–V7–Imaj7, IVmaj7–iii7–vi7, and a passing V7/ii. **(engine default; sources only establish "gospel-influenced piano") [38]**
- UKG: minor keys; chords chosen to "conjure positive emotions", i.e. maj7/add9 colours on VI and III. [13][35]

### Melody / hooks
- Vocal-chop hook (UKG/garage): 1–3-syllable synthetic "vowel" grains, re-pitched to chord tones and placed on swung 16ths; reverse 1 grain in 8. [4][34][35]
- Chicago hook: a 1–2-bar piano or synth riff repeated without change for 16–32 bars. [1][42]
- Piano house: M1-style bright piano chords playing the full progression on syncopated 8ths and 16ths, doubled an octave down. ("16'" layer, inferred from the preset name.) [21][41][46]
- French house hook: the filtered "loop bus" (§5) is the hook; no separate lead in 60%+ of tracks. **(engine default)** [3][32]
- Deep house: chords and bass are the hook; melodic lead optional (p≈0.3). **(engine default)** [2]

### Sound design
- Piano/organ: M1-style PCM-like piano and "Organ 2"-style organ as presets; the filter is non-resonant. [21][22]
- Pads/stabs: Juno-style poly (saw + pulse, chorus on) for deep house chords and Chicago stabs. [2][16][25]
- French house bus: resonant low-pass (cutoff automated 200 Hz → 12 kHz) + phaser + kick-keyed sidechain (ratio ≥10:1, release ~1/8 note). **(numbers are engine defaults; the technique is from [3][28][29])**
- Global house pump: sidechain the music bus from the kick, 2–6 dB in deep and Chicago house, 6–12 dB in French house. **(engine default)** [28]
- Lo-fi option for Chicago: 8-bit/6-bit crush on hats and cymbals, light tape. [6][19][20]
- Deep house: ambient reverb on chords and percussion, soft pads. [2]

### Arrangement & energy
- Build in 8-bar units; every section length is a multiple of 8 bars. [1][40]
- Progressive house: add or remove one layer every 4, 8 or 16 bars; the main build may run up to ~4 min. [40]
- DJ intro: 16–32 bars of drums (plus bass after 8–16 bars); mirror it in a 16–32-bar DJ outro. [13][44]
- Drop placement: first drop at bar 17–33 (NI places it at bar 25). [13][44]
- Silence: cut all drums for exactly 1 bar before a drop (p=0.5 per drop). [13]
- Outro: remove the bass for the last 16–32 bars. [13]
- Deep house: no big build/release; at most one breakdown, and energy changes by layer muting only. [2]
- Chicago/acid: long tracks (6–12 min) with slow layer changes. [1][18]
- French house: open the filter on the loop bus over 8–16 bars (unverified range). [3]

### Variation / surprise
- Turnaround kick on step 15 of the last bar in each 4- or 8-bar phrase, at 60–70% velocity. [5][6]
- Low tom on step 15 of odd bars, mid toms 2–3 steps earlier. [7]
- UKG: clap/rim fill at the end of every 2nd bar at 60–70% velocity. [14]
- UKG: a double kick before the downbeat (step 16 + next step 1, or steps 1–2), the second quieter, once per 4 bars. [9][14]
- 2-step: drop kick 1 of bar 2 with p=0.3. [10]
- Crash on bar 1 of each new section. [9]
- Speed garage: spin-back (rewind) or reversed-drum transition once per track. [4]
- Vocal chop: reverse or pitch-shift one syllable per 2 bars. [4][34]
- Filter house: one "filter dip" (cutoff to <400 Hz) for 1–2 bars before a section change, p=0.5. **(engine default)** [3]

# Trance (German, Goa/psy, uplifting, progressive, hard) and eurobeat

Research notes for the procedural music engine's trance family and for
eurobeat. Every claim is cited to the numbered references at the end. Where a
source gives a feel but no number, the text says so. Rules the sources do not
back are engine design choices and are marked **(engine default)**. Anything
I could not verify is marked **(unverified)**. Step numbers are 1–16 within one
4/4 bar of 16th notes: the beats are steps 1, 5, 9 and 13, and the off-beat
8ths are 3, 7, 11 and 15.

Source quality, plainly: the production detail for **psytrance** is good (a
four-part 2024 Bonedo workshop [22]–[25]). For **uplifting trance** it comes
from two Sound On Sound interviews ([3] Darren Tate, [4] Armin van Buuren),
Wikipedia and its cited books, and per-track Hooktheory transcriptions
([29]–[34], user-contributed, so treat them as samples rather than
statistics). For **the supersaw** there is a measured analysis of the
hardware ([5], building on Szabo [6]). **Eurobeat** has the thinnest
production literature. Its tempo, octave bass and riff-centred form are
sourced ([35]–[37], [40]), but its key-change habit rests on one genre blurb
and a general J-pop convention ([40], [42]).

## 2. Early German trance (Frankfurt, Berlin, 1990–94)

### Origins and landmarks
- Trance grew out of the techno and EBM scene in Frankfurt in the late 1980s and early 1990s, then spread across Europe [1]. Sven Väth opened the Omen club in Frankfurt in 1988 [13]. He founded Eye Q with Heinz Roth and Matthias Hoffmann in 1991, and Harthouse in 1992 [13].
- Eye Q released the softer side: Cygnus X "The Orange Theme", Brainchild "Symmetry", Vernon "Wonderer". Harthouse released the harder side: Spicelab "Quicksand", Metal Master "Spectrum", Resistance D "Human", Hardfloor "Acperience" [1].
- In Berlin, MFS (1991) signed Mijk van Dijk, Cosmic Baby and Paul van Dyk. It released Humate "Love Stimulation" and the *Tranceformed From Beyond* compilation [1].
- Dag Lerner (Dance 2 Trance) says he was the first to call the music "trance" [1].

### Sound
- The early style is long, hypnotic and 303-heavy. Hardfloor stacked "up to six" TB-303s [14]. Exact tempos of the landmark tracks are **(unverified)**. The genre's general range is 120–150 BPM [1].
- Väth credited the inspiration to Goa DJs using psychedelic rock to induce trance at beach parties [1].

## 3. Goa trance and psytrance

### Origins and landmarks
- Hippies arrived in Goa in the mid-1960s. Goa DJs played psychedelic rock in the 1970s, and electronic music arrived in 1979. From 1983, DJs Laurent and Fred Disko, then Goa Gil, moved the style toward EBM and Eurobeat [19].
- The first parties were at Bamboo Forest, Disco Valley and Arambol beach, c. 1991–93 [18]. From 1993, Goa-style parties spread abroad, through Return to the Source and Megatripolis in London [18][19]. Paul Oakenfold's 1994 "Goa Mix" Essential Mix pushed the sound [18].
- *Project II Trance* (1993, with Man With No Name and Hallucinogen) is called the first Goa trance album [19]. The first wave's golden age was 1994–97, and the commercial peak 1996–97 [19].
- Dark psytrance became a recognisable genre after 2003 in Germany and Russia [19]. Full-on's golden age was 2004–09 [26]. Progressive psy emerged in Europe in the mid-2000s [26].

### Tempo
- Goa: 130–150 BPM typical, with some tracks at 110–160 [18]. Psytrance in general: 125–150 [19]. Full-on: 140–148 [19][26]. Dark psy: from ~150 and "often faster" [19].
- A community guide's finer split [26]: progressive psy 130–140, "offbeat"/Hamburg 134–140, tribal 136–140, "psytrance" 138–144, full-on 140–148, night full-on 145–150, uptempo/UK psy 145–155, forest 150–160, hi-tech 170–200.

### Drums (16-step grid) [22]
- Kick on every quarter (1, 5, 9, 13). It is a sine with a fast pitch-down sweep, **no longer than an 8th note**. Volume dips just after the transient to thin out 200–600 Hz. "Classic" kicks use a lower sweep start and a slower initial drop. "Modern" kicks sweep faster and are layered with clicks or short hats.
- Main snare/clap on beats 2 and 4 (steps 5, 13): a 909 snare plus a clap plus a stereo snare, with no low end.
- Open hat on the 8th between kicks (3, 7, 11, 15). Closed hats replace it in lower-energy sections. Top loops are short 16th-note closed hats.
- Crash at the start or end of a 16-bar passage.

### Bass: the rolling KBBB [22][27][48]
- **Pattern:** kick on the beat, then three 16th-note bass hits (K B B B). Per bar: K on 1, 5, 9, 13; B on 2–4, 6–8, 10–12, 14–16. Each bass note is one 16th long [22]; forum practice says ideally *shorter* than a 16th, with no overlap [27].
- **Accent:** the first B after each K is quieter, so the ear does not hear it as a downbeat [22][27]. The amount is a taste parameter; no number is given.
- **Kick–bass seam:** the kick tail overlaps the first bass 16th, ideally on the same root frequency. Fix phase clashes by nudging tempo, inverting polarity, or removing the bass fundamental [22].
- **Synthesis:** a retriggered sawtooth through a low-pass filter. Amp: instant attack, full sustain, very short release. The filter envelope opens fully at note start and closes "within a few milliseconds", giving a pluck [22][27]. Classic basses: free-running phase (each hit slightly different) and a slower filter envelope. Modern basses: reset phase (identical hits) [22].
- **Variants:** off-beat bass (one 8th after the kick, one 8th long) in progressive psy [22]. Hamburg "offbeat" puts a single note between the kicks [26]. Tribal psy uses triplet basslines [26]. Full-on's bass "plays on various notes across a few octaves" rather than one repeated note [26].

### Synths, melody and FX [23][24][26]
- Modal scales (Dorian, Phrygian, Lydian) set the mood [23]. Goa melodies are described as "oriental", with Indian consonance [26]. A "Phrygian dominant" default is **(unverified)**.
- Leads are often monotone 16th-note step sequences. The rhythm comes from note jumps, and the filter cutoff opens slowly to add energy [23].
- "Fractal" sounds: sample & hold drives a resonant band-pass cutoff at a 16th-note rate on a saw [23].
- FM leads are fast and mostly monotone, shaped by high-pass filter movement. "Grids" are short FM shots, chord stabs (second oscillator +7 semitones; supersaws most common) and plucks [23].
- FX are built mainly from filter sweeps and pitch modulation, with long ping-pong delays. Risers are white noise with volume and filter modulation, layered with rising-pitch synths [24]. Delays are dotted quarter or 8th ping-pong [23].
- Full-on changes synth lines regularly. Progressive psy avoids frequent changes and modulates existing lines instead [24].

### Arrangement [19][25]
- Track length 6–10 min [19]; 7–9 min [25]; Goa 8–12 min [18].
- Leads, rhythms and beats change every 8 bars. New ideas layer in every 4–8 bars up to a climax, then a breakdown [19].
- Build-ups are 8, 16 or 32 bars, using risers, accelerating snare rolls, pitch automation, high- and low-pass sweeps on kick and bass, and sometimes a speeding kick. About 4 beats of pause (maybe a fill) come before the drop [25].
- Drops are made of 16-bar sections separated by ~4-beat breaks. At the start of a drop the percussion is stripped, then added back in order: closed hat, then snare, then open hat [25].
- The main breakdown sits about two-thirds through and lasts up to 64 bars [25], or 30 s to over a minute [19]. Simple breaks are ≤16 bars, with kick and bass resting [25]. The main part has 2–3 drops [25].
- The outro reduces to kick and bass for DJ mixing [25].

## 4. Uplifting / epic trance

### Origins and landmarks
- The name "uplifting" emerged in 1996 [17]. Uplifting dominated by the late 1990s; pop vocals arrived in the early 2000s [1]. Armin van Buuren's *A State of Trance* radio show started in 2001, and Armada was founded in 2003 [2].

### Tempo
- Uplifting: 135–140 BPM [17]. Recent uplifting forms: 120–135 [1]. Armin noted a falling BPM trend by 2009 but gave no figure [4]. Hooktheory tempo samples: 138 ("For an Angel") [29], 138 ("Children") [30], 132 ("9 PM") [31], 140 ("Out of the Blue") [33], 128 (Above & Beyond "Sun and Moon") [32].

### Drums
- Kick on every downbeat and open hat on the upbeat. The kick is de-emphasised compared with house and techno, to make room for the bass [1].
- Darren Tate starts from a "standard 909-ish kick" and closed hats [3], with the kick's chunk "at around 100Hz" and the deepest sub rolled off [3]. Armin puts the kick "around 60-70 Hz with a little top 'tick'" [4].
- Claps are made from white noise, compressed hard into a "blisteringly sharp clap", in stereo with reverb [3]. Clap on 2 and 4 (steps 5, 13) **(engine default, consistent with [22])**.
- Crash "on the first beat of the bar" as an accent; long reverb on crashes, short reverb on hats [3].

### Bass
- Off-beat bass: one note on every off-beat 8th (3, 7, 11, 15); keep the kick short so it doesn't overlap [43]. Armin's verse bass is "more straight" than the theme's [4]. He layers a sine under it for warmth and ducks it under the kick with sidechain compression [4].
- Tate pumps by keying the compressor from a separate MIDI pattern rather than the kick, compressing drum reverbs "on every beat" [3].
- Background strings or synths get volume automation that makes a "pulsing off-beat effect" [17]: a pad ducked on each beat, sounding on the off-beats.

### Signature sound: the supersaw [5][6][7][8]
- The Roland JP-8000 came out in 1996/early 1997 [7]. Its Super Saw "became a genre-defining aspect of trance", and supersaw leads defined "anthem trance" [7]. The JP-8000/8080, Access Virus and Novation Nova are Tate's trance staples [3].
- **Seven saws**: one fixed centre oscillator plus six detuned slaves, three either side [5].
- **Measured spread at maximum detune**, relative to the centre (C5 = 523.25 Hz): 0.8900, 0.9371, 0.9698, 1.0, 1.0295, 1.0622, 1.1075 [5]. That is about −202, −113, −53, 0, +50, +104, +177 cents (my conversion). A forum measurement gives a similar .893/.939/.980/1/1.020/1.064/1.110 [8]. Common musical settings are said to be 40–90 cents **(unverified, forum)** [8].
- **The detune knob is non-linear.** Shore's 17-point table runs 0.0097, 0.022, 0.034, 0.047, 0.059, 0.071, 0.084, 0.097, 0.122, 0.147, 0.193, 0.243, 0.293, 0.343, 0.393, then jumps to 1 [5]. The curve rises slowly and then sharply, so the low half of the knob gives string-like pads [5].
- **The mix knob crossfades.** The centre oscillator goes linearly from 1.0 to 0.445. The six side oscillators rise non-linearly, from 0.038 to a peak of ~0.605 at about 85% of the knob, then 0.59 at full [5].
- **Free-running/random phase** per note is "perhaps the most important thing" [8], and was measured by Szabo [5].
- **24 dB/oct high-pass filter, key-tracked to the fundamental**, at the end of the chain [5][8].
- Armin describes the classic sound as "the very clean Super Saw, a little detune, a little delay", against later distortion-heavy leads [4].

### Pads, gates, arps
- Pads are sustained legato chords, mostly in breakdowns ("floating universe") [5]. Trance pads are multi-saw, string-like, with long reverb, filling the mids and highs [5].
- **Trance gate:** a pad chopped rhythmically. Originally a noise gate keyed by a hi-hat sequence on its sidechain [44]. Hard trance uses gated pads [1]. A 16-step pattern is the norm in software gates [44]. Specific classic patterns are **(unverified)**.
- Arpeggiated saw or square leads sit in the background, with synth choirs or strings in front [17]. Rapid arpeggios are common [1].

### Harmony
- Minor keys are described as "almost universal" [1], **(citation needed in source)**. Uplifting is said to lean on longer runs of major chords [17]. Read together: minor tonic, mostly major chords in the progression (VI, III, VII).
- Hooktheory samples (user-transcribed, single section each):

|---|---|---|
| "For an Angel" [29] | A minor | i – v – VII (Am–Em–G) |
| "Children" [30] | F minor | VI – iv – i (D♭–B♭m–Fm) |
| "9 PM (Till I Come)" [31] | A minor | i – iv – III (Am–Dm–C) |

- The classic minor "Humoresque" loop is i–VI–VII–III. Heard in the relative major it is vi–IV–V–I [45]. The i–VI–III–VII order the brief asks for is the same chord set in a different order. I found no corpus count of which order trance prefers, so treat both as generator options **(engine default)**.
- Suspended and added-note colour appears in the transcriptions: "sus2" and "add9" in "Out of the Blue" [33], "i9" in "Children" [30]. A forum source says uplifting often swaps v for a major V (raised 7th) for a stronger cadence **(forum)** [45].
- Chords and melody are the producer's priority, and they must be "quite straightforward", catchy on first hearing [3].

### Melody and hooks
- One central hook runs through the track, repeating at intervals from 2 beats to 32 bars [1].
- Armin starts from an 8-bar main theme (piano or strings over drums and bass), then an 8-bar "verse" variation with a straighter bass, then an 8-bar "chorus". He ends with "four, five, six different sections" [4].
- "For an Angel" chorus melody: range G4–C5 (5 semitones), stepwise, ~0.8 beats per note, 100% diatonic, 61% chord tones [29]. The hook is narrow and stepwise; the size comes from the sound.
- Before the breakdown, a sliced-up, simplified version of the lead is introduced as a teaser. The final climax combines earlier material with the main melody [1].
- Vocals: mostly female mezzo-soprano to soprano, usually without verse/chorus structure [1].
- Call-and-response between two lead phrases: no source found **(unverified)**.

### Arrangement and energy arc
- Club mix 7–8 minutes; radio edit ~3.5 minutes [3]. Long, sparse intros and outros for DJ mixing [1]. Work starts as 4- or 8-bar sections [3].
- 4/4 in 16- or 32-beat phrases. Instruments enter or leave every 4, 8, 16 or 32 bars [1].
- One or two breakdowns. The breakdown is the "hands in the air" moment, "and then it builds and builds and builds"; build length is by ear [3]. Tension peaks in 1–2 drops [1].
- Build tools: snare rolls, filtered white-noise "whoosh", a pad filter opening [3]. Armin duplicates the drum track to build tension toward the chorus [4].
- A school (AQA) trance template: 32-bar build, 16-bar breakdown, 16-bar full-texture drop [46]. This is a teaching template, not an analysis.
- **Snare-roll grammar:** Attack's "speed-up roll" is 4 quarters, then 4 8ths, then 8 16ths in one bar at constant tempo [28]. Further layers are a velocity ramp from 1 to 127, a pitch rise across the bar, a filter rising to fully open at the drop, and a roll that stops early (at beat 2.4) for a pre-drop gap [28]. Psy builds use "accelerating snare rolls" [25]. Stretching the same quarter → 8th → 16th → 32nd doubling over 8 or 16 bars is **(engine default)**.

## 5. Progressive trance / house (Sasha, Digweed)

- Progressive house came from the UK in the early 1990s. Leftfield's "Not Forgotten" (October 1990) may be the first [16]. Mixmag's 1992 list includes Slam, Gat Decor "Passion" and Spooky "Don't Panic" [16]. The name was often used interchangeably with trance early on [16].
- *Renaissance: The Mix Collection* (1994) and *Northern Exposure* (1996), Sasha & John Digweed's mix albums, established the genre [16]. Sasha's "Xpander" (1999) is a landmark [2]. Labels: Guerilla, Deconstruction, Hooj Choons, Soma, Bedrock [16].
- Tempo: progressive trance 126–132 BPM [2].
- Intensity comes from adding and removing layers. Phrases are 4, 8 or 16 bars and often start with a new melody or rhythm. Later tracks build for up to four minutes, then break down and climax [16].
- It lacks "anthemic choruses, crescendos, and drum rolls" [16]. The generator should use filter and layer builds, not snare rolls.

## 6. Hard trance

- Belgium, Germany and the Netherlands, early 1990s, with roots in new beat, hardcore and hard house. Popular in mainland Europe 1993–97, declining by the mid-2000s [15].
- Bonzai Records (Belgium): Jones & Stephenson "The First Rebirth" (1993), Yves Deruyter "Rave City" (1993), Cherrymoon Trax "Let There Be House" (1994), Phrenetic System "Back to Reality" (1994) [15].
- Tempo 140–180 BPM [15] (a wide band; the low end is the trance-adjacent part). Hard or down-pitched kicks, "fully resonant" basses, more reverb on the main beat, later side-chaining [15]. Faster tempo, gated pads, and the breakdown–build–anthem template [1].
- A rolling 16th bass (a note on every 16th) "will clash hard with your kick" unless ducked [43].

## 7. Eurobeat

### Origins and landmarks
- In Japan, "Eurobeat" was first applied to Italo-disco imports from the mid-1980s. Mauro Farina, who supplied Alfa Music, coined the name [35]. *That's Eurobeat* ran in Japan from 1986 to 1994 [36]. Dead or Alive's "You Spin Me Round" (1984) is cited as establishing the FM-synth octave bassline as a defining trait [36].
- A-Beat-C was founded in 1990 by Giancarlo Pasquini (Dave Rodgers) and Alberto Contini, with Alessandra Mirka Gatti (Domino) and Avex's Max Matsuura and Tom Yoda [35]. Delta Music Industry was founded in 1995 by Clara Moroni and Laurent Newfield [41]. A-Beat-C, Time and Delta were credited by Avex for the success of Super Eurobeat Vol. 100 [35].
- *Super Eurobeat* started in 1990 under Avex [35][36][39]. One source says 1991 [37]. It was monthly, then bimonthly until 2018, then yearly [39]. Vol. 100 reached Oricon No. 2 (August 1999) and Vol. 110 No. 1 (August 2000) [36].
- *Initial D* First Stage aired April–December 1998 [47]. Its Eurobeat soundtrack is a major reason for the genre's continued popularity [36][38]. Para Para booms came in the mid-1980s, early 1990s and 1999–2001. The third was helped by Takuya Kimura dancing to "Night of Fire" on TV [37].

### Tempo
- 150–160 BPM [37][40]. Japanese Wikipedia: 120–160 overall, around 125 for 1980s Eurobeat, many tracks ≥140 after Super Eurobeat [36]. Hooktheory's "Deja Vu" lists 145 and 154 BPM in different sections. This is probably a transcription inconsistency **(unverified)** [34].

### Drums
- Four-on-the-floor [40] and "tight percussion" [35]. Snare/clap on 2 and 4 and off-beat open hats are **(engine default; no eurobeat-specific source)**.

### Bass
- Synth bass in **8th notes, played in octaves** ("octave technique") [36]: a "sequenced octave bass" [35]. On the grid: the low root on 1, 5, 9, 13 and the octave above on 3, 7, 11, 15, following chord roots. The exact low/high order is **(engine default)**. The genre is also described as having "off-beat basslines" [40]. The octave bass puts its high note on the off-beat, so the two descriptions agree.

### Sound
- Heavy synths, brass and harp sounds; electric guitar more common from the mid-1990s [35]. 1990s gear: Yamaha SY/EOS, Roland JD-800/990 [36]. Everything except guitar and vocals is programmed [36].
- The **synth riff** (often called the "sabi" in the English fandom) is roughly **8 bars** long [36]. It recurs, is weighed equally with verse, bridge and chorus [36], and can stack up to 20 tracks [35]. In J-pop usage "sabi" (サビ) means the chorus, so the term is ambiguous [36]. I use "riff" here.

### Harmony
- "Key-change climaxes" [40]. Modulating up a half or whole step in the final chorus is a general J-pop convention [42]. Hooktheory's "Deja Vu" shows an A-minor intro and C-minor verse/chorus [34] (also an inconsistent transcription).
- "Deja Vu" progressions [34]: chorus VI–VII–i–VII–VI; verse i–VI–VII–i–v. The minor-key VI–VII–i rise is the same chord family as trance's.

### Form
- Intro → riff → A-melo (verse) → B-melo (bridge) → chorus → riff → (second A/B/chorus with new lyrics) → ending [35]. The riff follows the intro and each chorus [35]. Most songs have two verse–bridge–chorus cycles [35]. English vocals, singers working under aliases [35][37].

## Rules and parameters

### Tempo & feel
- Early German/acid trance: 128–140 BPM **(engine default within 120–150 [1])**. [1][14]
- Goa: 135–150 BPM; psytrance 138–144; full-on 140–148; night full-on 145–150; dark psy 148–155+. [18][19][26]
- Progressive psy: 130–138 BPM; progressive trance: 126–132 BPM. [2][26]
- Uplifting/epic: 136–140 BPM, centre 138; "modern uplifting" option 128–135. [1][17][29][30][33]
- Hard trance: 140–150 BPM by default; allow up to 160. [15] (180 ceiling in [15] belongs to adjacent hardcore styles)
- Eurobeat: 150–160 BPM, centre 155. [36][37][40]
- All trance styles: straight 16ths, swing 0% **(engine default)**; allow tribal-psy triplet bass as an option. [26]
- 4/4 throughout; phrases of 16 or 32 beats (4 or 8 bars). [1]

### Drums
- Kick on every quarter (steps 1, 5, 9, 13) in every style here. [1][22][40]
- Open hat on the off-beat 8ths (3, 7, 11, 15); swap to closed hats in lower-energy sections. [1][22]
- Clap/snare on 5 and 13; build trance claps from white noise with a very short decay and heavy compression, plus reverb. [3][22]
- 16th closed-hat top loop on all 16 steps in psy at mid/high energy. [22]
- Psy kick: sine, fast downward pitch sweep, length ≤ 1/8 note, with a dip just after the click (cut 200–600 Hz). [22]
- Uplifting kick: 909-style, body around 60–100 Hz plus a short "tick"; high-pass the sub below it. [3][4]
- Crash on step 1 of the first bar of each 16-bar block; long reverb on crashes, short on hats. [3][22]

### Bass
- Psytrance: KBBB. Kick on 1/5/9/13, bass on the other twelve 16ths; each note ≤ 1 step long, no overlap. [22][27]
- Make the first B after each K 2–4 dB quieter **(engine default amount; direction from [22][27])**.
- Psy bass patch: saw → LPF; amp attack 0, release ≤ 10 ms; filter env opens fully and decays in ~5–30 ms **(engine default numbers; shape from [22][27])**. Random oscillator phase for "classic", reset phase for "modern". [22]
- Tune the kick tail to the bass root; keep the root in E–G for psy **(engine default)**. [22]
- Full-on: allow the bass to move across 2–3 octaves within a bar. [26]
- Progressive psy / Hamburg: off-beat bass, one 8th note on 3, 7, 11, 15. [22][26]
- Uplifting / German trance: off-beat bass on 3, 7, 11, 15, sidechain-ducked by the kick or a MIDI-keyed pump on every beat. Layer a sine under it. [1][3][4][43]
- Hard trance: rolling bass on all 16 steps or off-beat; sidechain depth ≥ 6 dB when rolling **(engine default amount)**. [15][43]
- Eurobeat: 8th-note octave bass; low root on 1, 5, 9, 13 and the octave above on 3, 7, 11, 15; follow the chord root. [35][36][40]

### Harmony
- Trance tonic: minor in ≥ 80% of seeds **(engine default; [1] says "almost universal", uncited)**. Major tonic option for "Out of the Blue"-style anthems. [1][33]
- Uplifting chord pool (minor key): i, iv, v, VI, III, VII; weight VI, III, VII high so most chords are major. [17][29][30][31][32]
- Loops to draw from: i–VI–III–VII, i–VI–VII–III, VI–VII–i, VI–iv–i, i–v–VII, i–iv–III, i–III–iv; one chord per bar or per 2 bars **(rate is engine default)**. [29][30][31][32][45]
- Add sus2, add9 and min9 colour on 25–40% of chord changes in uplifting **(engine default rate; colour from [30][33])**.
- Optional major V (raised 7th) before i at phrase ends in uplifting. [45] (forum)
- Psy: modal single-chord or drone harmony; pick Phrygian, Dorian or Lydian; Goa leans "oriental" (Phrygian). [18][23][26]
- Eurobeat: minor key; verse i–VI–VII–i–v, chorus VI–VII–i–VII. [34]
- Eurobeat key change: modulate the last chorus up 1 or 2 semitones in 60–80% of songs **(engine default rate; direction from [40][42])**.

### Melody / hooks
- One main hook, 8 bars long, reused across the track; repeat units from 2 beats to 32 bars. [1][4]
- Uplifting hook: narrow range (≤ 7 semitones), stepwise, ~1 note per beat, all diatonic, ~60% chord tones. [29]
- Before the main breakdown, play a sliced, simplified version of the hook (every other note, or only its first 2 bars) as a teaser. [1]
- The final drop combines the full hook with earlier material (arp, bass, counter-line). [1]
- Psy leads: 16th-note step sequences, mostly one pitch with octave or 5th jumps; open the filter slowly over 8–16 bars. [23]
- Eurobeat: an 8-bar synth riff after the intro and after every chorus; vocal-style top line in the verses and chorus. [35][36]

### Sound design
- Supersaw: 7 saws; spread ratios at max detune 0.890, 0.937, 0.970, 1, 1.030, 1.062, 1.107 (scale them by the detune curve). [5]
- Supersaw detune knob: use the measured non-linear 17-point table (≈0.01 → 0.39 over points 1–15, then 1 and 2 at points 16–17). Default lead detune ≈ 0.10–0.25 of the max spread. [5] (default amount is engine default)
- Supersaw mix: centre gain 1.0 → 0.445 linear; side gain 0.04 → 0.60 concave; default mix about 60–75%. [5] (default is engine default)
- Supersaw: random start phase per voice per note; key-tracked 24 dB/oct high-pass at the fundamental. [5][8]
- Lead FX: "a little delay" (1/8 or dotted 1/8 ping-pong) plus long reverb; mostly clean, distortion optional for post-2005 styles. [4][23]
- Pads: multi-saw strings, slow attack, long release and reverb; mostly in breakdowns. [5]
- Trance gate: 16-step on/off pattern on the pad, 50–75% steps on, each gate ≤ one 16th **(pattern density is engine default; technique [1][44])**.
- Uplifting off-beat pumping: duck pads and strings on every beat (1, 5, 9, 13), 4–8 dB, recovering by the off-beat. [3][17] (amount is engine default)
- Acid line (early German, Goa): 303 voice with resonance and envelope modulation; stack up to 2 lines. [14][18]
- Psy FM: 4-op FM "shots" and leads; more modulation index = more aggressive; high-pass sweep for movement. [23]
- Psy "fractal": sample & hold on a resonant band-pass cutoff at a 16th rate. [23]
- Psy FX: filter sweeps, pitch dives, long ping-pong delays; white-noise risers. [24]
- Eurobeat palette: FM octave bass, brass stabs, harp/bell arps, bright lead, optional overdriven guitar. [35][36]

### Arrangement & energy
- Uplifting club mix: 7–8 min. Layout **(engine default bar counts, built on [1][3][25][46])**: intro 32 bars of drums only, with bass entering at bar 17 → build 16 → main section 32 (hook teaser at its end) → breakdown 32–64 (pads plus hook alone, no kick) → build 8–16 → drop 32 → outro 32.
- Change something every 8 bars; never let a layer set run longer than 32 bars. [1][19]
- Psy: 7–9 min. Build-ups 8, 16 or 32 bars; ~4 beats of silence or a fill before each drop. Drops are 16-bar blocks with ~4-beat gaps between them. Main breakdown at ~2/3 of the track, ≤ 64 bars. Short breaks ≤ 16 bars with no kick or bass. 2–3 drops. [25]
- Psy drop entry: start with kick and bass only; add closed hat at +16 bars, snare at +32, open hat at +48. [25]
- Progressive: no snare rolls; build by filters and layers for up to 4 minutes (≈ 128 bars at 128 BPM). [16]
- Snare build over the last 8 bars before a drop: quarters for 2 bars → 8ths for 2 → 16ths for 2 → 32nds for 1 → silence for 1 **(engine default stretch of the 1-bar speed-up roll in [28])**.
- During the build: velocity ramp 1 → 127, pitch up 3–12 semitones, high-pass or low-pass sweep fully open at the drop, white-noise riser. [3][28]
- Option (≈20%): stop the roll at beat 2.4 of the last bar for a dramatic gap. [28]
- Eurobeat: ~3.5–5 min. Intro 8–16 → riff 8 → A 8 → B 8 → chorus 16 → riff 8 → A 8 → B 8 → chorus 16 → (key change) chorus 16 → riff 8 → ending 8 **(bar counts engine default; order from [35][36])**.

### Variation / surprise
- A second breakdown is optional (one or two per track). [3]
- Drop variant: swap the drop's first 16 bars to a "stripped" start (kick and bass only) or a "full" start that thins out instead. [25]
- Psy: re-roll the lead every 8 bars in full-on; in progressive psy keep the lead and modulate its filter, phaser or pitch instead. [24]
- Uplifting: on the second pass of the chord loop, start on a different chord of the same loop (e.g. VI instead of i). [45] (forum)
- Eurobeat: final-chorus key lift (+1 or +2 semitones) as the main surprise; riff re-voiced with an extra layer each time it returns. [35][40][42]
- Rare surprise (≤10%): a real-instrument hook in place of the synth lead (guitar à la ATB "9 PM" or Infected Mushroom "Becoming Insane"). [10][20]

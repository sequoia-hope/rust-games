# Synthwave / outrun, electro, Italo disco and their roots

Scope: the 1977–1987 sources (Moroder and Hi-NRG, Italo disco, the synth film and TV scores of Vangelis, Tangerine Dream, Jan Hammer and John Carpenter), electro (Kraftwerk to "Planet Rock" to Drexciya), and the 2005–2020s revival (synthwave, outrun, retrowave, darksynth). The aim is generator parameters, so every section ends in numbers.

**Conventions.** Steps are 1–16 per 4/4 bar: step 1 = beat 1, 5 = beat 2, 9 = beat 3, 13 = beat 4, odd steps are 8ths, "offbeat 8ths" are steps 3, 7, 11 and 15. Roman numerals are relative to the tonic of the stated mode. Tempos marked [23] come from Hooktheory's user-made analyses and those marked [24] from SongBPM's algorithmic estimates. Both are approximate, and either can report double time (for example 171 for a track that feels like 85.5).

## 2. The roots

### 2.1 Giorgio Moroder, "I Feel Love" (1977), and Hi-NRG

- **Origins.** "I Feel Love" was recorded at Musicland, Munich, and released on 2 July 1977. It was produced by Moroder and Pete Bellotte and recorded to 16-track tape [4][5]. Moroder built the backing track and bassline *before* the melody: "I do the tracks before," "I started with the bass line" [7].
- **The bassline mechanism.** A borrowed Moog modular played a sequence synced to a click recorded on track 16 of the tape. The Moog drifted out of tune, so it was recorded in bursts of 20–30 s [4][5][6]. Every bass note is doubled by a delay: the dry line is panned left and the delayed copy right, which makes a "flickering, strobe-like" pulse [4]. The original was an **8-step sequence**. The offset echo makes it sound like 16 steps [8]. **Generator reading: 8th-note sequence plus one delayed copy at a 1/16 offset, hard-panned.**
- **Other parts.** The hi-hat was Moog white noise shaped by an envelope [4][6]. The kick was the one live drum: Keith Forsey played "seven minutes of four-to-the-floor thump", because the Moog gave "oomph" instead of "dum" [6][7]. The four-on-the-floor kick with an offbeat hat became the template for house and techno [4].
- **Harmony.** The Hooktheory chorus analysis is C major, I–♭III–IV–V, at 126 BPM [23]. SongBPM gives 128 BPM for an 8:14 version [24].
- **Hi-NRG** runs at 120–140 BPM. Its signature is "pulsating octave basslines, often sequenced, sometimes taking the place of the hi-hat", with staccato hats, four-on-the-floor kicks and heavy drum-machine clap [9]. Dead or Alive's "You Spin Me Round" is F# Dorian, 128 BPM: verse i–IV, chorus i–III–VII–i–IV [23][24].

### 2.2 Italo disco

- **Origins.** European and mostly Italian producers kept disco going after the US backlash of 1979. "Italo" appeared on West German compilations by 1978, and ZYX marketed it abroad from 1982 [10]. Cheap synths and drum machines replaced the US disco orchestra. Fred Ventura has said the minimal productions were often a budget choice [11]. By 1983 the instrumentation was mainly electronic, with occasional vocoders, accented English vocals, and themes of love, robots and space [10]. Around 1987 it turned into Italo house, and later into Eurobeat for Japan [10].
- **Tempos and keys from the sample.** Laura Branigan "Self Control": F# minor, 107 BPM, chorus i–v. Ryan Paris "Dolce Vita": E♭ major, 120–122 BPM, verse ii–V–I. Baltimora "Tarzan Boy": F major, 108–110 BPM, I–V–vi–IV–V. Gazebo "I Like Chopin": 103 BPM, 8:56 version [23][24].
- **A worked Italo groove** (Native Instruments, 120 BPM, E minor) [11]:
  - kick on every beat (1, 5, 9, 13) plus a softer extra kick (velocity 80 against 100) halfway through the last beat of bar 1 (step 15);
  - claps on 5 and 13;
  - closed hats on the offbeat 8ths (3, 7, 11, 15), switching to an open hat on the last offbeat of the 2-bar loop;
  - bass and lead from one-bar sequences;
  - chords E–D–C–D (I–♭VII–♭VI–♭VII) under a lead that avoids the minor third;
  - a counter-melody with 68% delay and 72% reverb;
  - elements added or removed every 4 or 8 bars, with a synth FX sweep at each section change.
- **"Problèmes d'Amour" (Alexander Robotnick, 1983), section by section** [12]:
  - Tempo drifts around 120.7 BPM because the TR-808 was the master clock.
  - It opens on bare 808 hats: closed hats on all 16 steps, open hats on 3, 7, 11 and 15, and a rimshot pattern panned left. The kick enters some bars into the first chorus.
  - The form is verse/chorus/verse "stretched beyond pop length": 4-bar hook, 4-bar verse, 8-bar choruses, a 12-bar middle eight.
  - The key is G minor. The middle eight modulates to B♭ minor (+3 semitones) and a synth solo moves to D major.
  - A 303 bassline changes pattern per section. Snare fills close each 8-bar block.
  - A 2-bar drums-only break ends in a reversed-reverb riser. A siren synth rises and falls in the instrumental break and the outro.
- **The Eurodance descendant** (Captain Hollywood Project, 1992): 909 four-on-the-floor kick, open hat on 3, 7, 11 and 15, claps on 5 and 13, a tambourine on all 16 steps, and a syncopated conga part "breaking up the rigid four-to-the-floor" [53].

### 2.3 The film and TV scores

- **John Carpenter, *Halloween* (1978).** Carpenter played a piano melody in 10/8 (described as a "complex 5/4") with synth programming by Dan Wyman. There is no orchestra, and the whole score was written in three days [33]. The theme is analysed in F# minor at 136–140 BPM, verse i–iv and chorus i–iv7–V [23]. Synthwave writers name Carpenter as a core influence [1][41].
- **Tangerine Dream.** Molly Lambert (MTV, 2016) called "Love on a Real Train" (*Risky Business*, 1983) a major influence, citing its "ornately repetitive synth patterns", "hypnotic chimes" and "percussive choogling drum machines" [1]. Hooktheory has it in F minor at 111 BPM, looping VI(add9)–VII–i7 [23]. SongBPM also gives 111 BPM [24].
- **Vangelis, *Blade Runner* (recorded Dec 1981–Apr 1982).** The main instrument was the Yamaha CS-80, with a Prophet-10, Jupiter-4, CR-5000 drum machine, GS1 FM and a Rhodes. Dick Morrissey played saxophone on "Love Theme" [32]. The CS-80 (1977) has 8 voices with two layers each, polyphonic aftertouch and a ribbon for polyphonic pitch bends [31]. These are the source of the "CS-80 lead": a slow-attack brass/lead swelling under aftertouch with a ribbon glide.
- **Jan Hammer, *Miami Vice* (1984–).** "Miami Vice Theme" reached No. 1 on the Billboard Hot 100 in 1985 [35]. "Crockett's Theme" reached No. 1 in Belgium and the Netherlands and No. 2 in the UK in 1987 [54]. Hammer's own description of his method (1986) [34]:
  - a single sound "dictates the feel of the piece";
  - drums, congas and occasional bass are sequenced on the Fairlight's Page R;
  - "the LinnDrum does the basics and the Fairlight does the funny stuff";
  - he then adds solos and chords, often through a valve amp and an MXR flanger, for a distorted lead he plays like a guitar using a portable keyboard and the pitch-bend Probe controller;
  - "a sequencer has a relentless feel to it — the repetition has a merciless effect";
  - the DX7 is a favourite "because of the way the upper harmonics pop up" with velocity.

  "Crockett's Theme" is analysed in D minor at 96 BPM, i–III–VII–♭II–vii [23]. "Miami Vice Theme" is about 116 BPM [24].
- **Harold Faltermeyer, "Axel F" (1984).** F minor, 117–120 BPM, i–VI7–VII–i [23][24].
- ***Out Run* (Sega, 1986).** Hiroshi Kawaguchi wrote the soundtrack, whose tracks ("Passing Breeze", "Splash Wave", "Magical Sound Shower") draw on Latin and Caribbean music. The "outrun" genre name and Kavinsky's album *OutRun* (2013) both come from the game [1][36].

## 3. Electro

- **Origins.** Electro appeared in Tokyo, New York and Detroit in the early 1980s, descended from Kraftwerk and YMO [13]. YMO were the first band to use the 808, at Budokan in 1980, and Sakamoto's "Riot in Lagos" (1980) took it into clubs [20]. 1982 was the watershed year ("Planet Rock", Warp 9's "Nunk"), and 1983 brought Hashim's "Al-Naafiysh", Herbie Hancock's "Rockit" and Cybotron's "Clear" [13]. There were revivals in the late 1990s (Anthony Rother, Dave Clarke), in 2007 and in 2016 (Helena Hauff, DJ Stingray) [13].
- **"Planet Rock" (1982).** Arthur Baker had found Kraftwerk's "Trans-Europe Express" too slow. He combined its melody with the beat of "Numbers" and Captain Sky's "Super Sporm" [14][15]. The 808 was hired with its programmer from a Village Voice ad ("Man with drum machine, 20 dollars a session") [14]. John Robie played a Micromoog and a Prophet-5, and a Fairlight supplied the "explosion" in the break and the "infamous orchestra" hit [14]. Baker added "probably the first rap record that had its own individual new chorus" [14]. Hooktheory analyses it in B Phrygian at 127 BPM, and SongBPM gives 126–127 BPM [23][24].
- **Kraftwerk.** "Computer World" (1981): G minor, 132 BPM, i–III–VII. "The Model": A minor, 124 BPM, i–v with a bridge in the parallel major [23].
- **Detroit/afrofuturist electro.** Drexciya (James Stinson, Gerald Donald; 1992–2002; *Bubble Metropolis* 1993, *Aquatic Invasion* 1994, *Neptune's Lair* 1999) built tracks on the TR-808 and TR-909 with a Roland D-20, Casio CZ-5000, Kawai K1 and Korg Mono/Poly. Their sound is "mechanical and fluid", with sonar-like pings and diving sounds [21]. "Wavejumper" is about 127 BPM [24]. Dopplereffekt is Gerald Donald's later project (from 1995; *Gesamtkunstwerk* 1999, *Linear Accelerator* 2003, *Calabi-Yau Space* 2007). It keeps scientific themes [22], and "Pornoactress" is about 128 BPM [24].
- **Characteristics.** Machine emulations of breakbeats have a syncopated kick under a backbeat snare or clap, and are "more mechanical" than live breaks. Vocals are vocoded or speech-synthesised, alongside sequenced or arpeggiated riffs, atonal FX, synthetic strings, and reverb, delay, chorus and phaser [13]. Programming style: "rigidly quantised, robotic feel", "no variation in velocity beyond… the 808's Accent", snare only on the backbeat "with no ghost notes", every kick and snare at full velocity, 16th hats with "a few holes punched in", the last snare of the phrase doubled with a clap, an open hat on the final 16th, and a busy syncopated cowbell kept for choruses or breakdowns [16].

**Lo-fi electro variant [19]:** 130–135 BPM, swing **55–65%** (Ableton "Swing 16-15"), TR-606/707 sounds. The kick plays a long note on each downbeat and a shorter one on the following offbeat. The snare is on every backbeat, tuned two octaves above the kick's pitch (kick about 52 Hz, snare about 210 Hz, both G#). Hats come in 16th bursts at the start of each bar. Bell percussion is syncopated and band-passed at 1 kHz with a slow LFO.

## 4. Synthwave / outrun / retrowave

### 4.1 Origins and key artists

- The genre emerged in France and Western Europe in the mid-to-late 2000s, led by David Grellier (College, of the Valerie collective) and Kavinsky (active from 2005). It grew out of French house and bloghouse and was first called "retro electro" [1][37].
- Rosso Corsa Records began online "in the early 2000s" with Michael Glover (Miami Nights 1984) and Garrett Hays (Lazerhawk). Its own name for the sound was "Outrun", described as "wild arpeggiated chords, highly-compressed dynamic ranges and big, Phil Collins-esque gated drums" [41]. Hays started by remaking Kano's Italo track "I'm Ready" with analog-synth VSTs, 1980s drum samples and vocoder, aiming to make the listener "feel like… the action hero" [41].
- Mitch Murder (Johan Bengtsson, Stockholm): *After Hours* EP (2009); *Burning Chrome* (2010) and *Current Events* (2011) on Rosso Corsa; *Interceptor* (2014) on Mad Decent; the *Kung Fury* score (2015). He cites Jan Hammer, Paul Hardcastle and Vince DiCola, and writes in the Renoise tracker [42].
- **The *Drive* soundtrack (2011)** brought the sound to a wide audience. It includes Kavinsky's "Nightcall" (2010), College & Electric Youth's "A Real Hero" and Desire's "Under Your Spell", plus Cliff Martinez's "retro, 80ish, synthesizer europop" score. It reached No. 1 on the US and UK soundtrack charts [1][40]. *Hotline Miami* (2012), *Far Cry 3: Blood Dragon* (2013) and *Stranger Things* spread the sound further [1].
- **Darksynth.** Perturbator (James Kent, Paris; active from 2012; *Dangerous Days* 2014, *The Uncanny Valley* 2016, *Lustful Sacraments* 2021) moved toward industrial, post-punk and goth, with "a lot of guitars" [43][44]. Carpenter Brut (Franck Hueso; *EP I–III* 2012–15 collected as *Trilogy*, *Leather Teeth* 2018) mixes horror, metal and "Italo disco revival" [45]. The subgenre swaps *Miami Vice* brightness for Carpenter/Goblin horror textures and EBM [1].
- **Dreamwave / pop end.** The Midnight (Tyler Lyle and Tim McEwan, formed 2012; *Endless Summer* 2016, *Nocturnal* 2017, *Kids* 2018, *Monsters* 2020, *Heroes* 2022) take as their motto "mono no aware", "a sense of nostalgic wistfulness" [46]. Their sound "recalls 1985 all the way down to the wailing sax solos and shimmering keytars" [48]. Timecop1983 (Jordy Leenaerts, Eindhoven; *Childhood Memories* 2014, *Night Drive* 2018) mixes synthwave with dream pop and melancholy songwriting [49][50]. The Weeknd's "Blinding Lights" (2020) took the synthwave-influenced sound to No. 1 in the US [1]. Hooktheory analyses it in F Dorian at 171 BPM (felt as about 85.5), i–v–VII–IV [23].
- FM-84 (Col Bennett) and Lazerhawk's *Redline* (2010) are canonical in fan histories, but I could not verify details beyond tempos (unverified).

### 4.3 Drums

- **Attack "Synthwave Drums" grid** (95–115 BPM, no swing, DrumTraks / Akai XR10 samples) [3]:
  - kick on **1 and 9** (beats 1 and 3) in both bars, plus a turnaround kick on **step 16 of bar 2**;
  - snare on **5 and 13**;
  - closed hat on all 16ths, "modulated slightly through velocity variations";
  - open hat **once per bar** (around step 14) "to provide impetus into the next" bar;
  - one **32nd-note hat** near the end of bar 2;
  - claps in bar 2 only, around **10, 11, 14 and 15**;
  - nonlinear (gated) reverb on kick and clap, mixed low on the kick.
- **ModeAudio** [2]:
  - kick and snare "on beats 1 and 3" (at the slow tempos, a half-time backbeat; at 80–118 BPM this is the same as kick on 1 and snare on 9);
  - closed hats filling the gaps;
  - "little or no velocity variation";
  - extra snare hits at the end of a bar as a fill into the next section, and "big, boomy toms" in fills;
  - an 808 cowbell for colour;
  - start the track with kick and snare only, bringing hats in later;
  - send most drums to a gated reverb, using algorithmic rather than convolution reverbs, with a gate after the reverb to cut the tails.
- **Timecop1983 variant.** "Drum tracks often eschew hi-hats and cymbals… rely solely on simple kick and snare patterns, sometimes with toms for fills", leaving space for the synths [50].
- **Outrun at 128+** uses a four-on-the-floor kick with a backbeat clap, inherited from Hi-NRG and Italo [9][11]. *A Real Hero*'s arpeggios "mainly accent the upbeats" over the beat [39].
- **Gated snare recipe** [25][26]:
  - a 100%-wet reverb send, with pre-delay 0;
  - gate hold of about 0.5 s, then a fast close;
  - compress the drum *before* the reverb so every hit triggers the gate evenly;
  - EQ the reverb return to remove boom;
  - separate gated reverbs per drum: shorter hold for hats and percussion, longer for kick and snare;
  - keep the gated part simple and avoid fast hats through it.

### 4.4 Bass

- **Root-repeating 8ths are the default.** "Sequenced basslines typically repeat the chord's root note on every beat or eighth note, creating a driving rhythm" [2].
- **"A Real Hero"** has "a chunky-sounding bass playing plucked 1/8th notes". The remake patch: Prophet-5 in mono unison, saw + square, osc 2 detuned 9 cents, cutoff 171 Hz, envelope amount 3.4, filter decay 490 ms (no attack or sustain), amp decay 660 ms with sustain 0.3, then saturation [39].
- **"Nightcall"**: the chorus bass is two saws an octave apart with the filter half-open (about 1.08 kHz), driven. A piano doubles the bass notes in octaves [38]. A separate resonant "sweep" mono patch also has saws an octave apart, cutoff about 727 Hz, high resonance, a 2.5 s filter decay sweeping down, and a **delayed vibrato and filter LFO at 5.29 Hz** that fades in as the note is held. It is layered with a sub saw low-passed at 216 Hz [38].
- **Timecop1983 plucked basses**: Juno saw plus sub oscillator (sub level 6/10 for a lighter tone, 10/10 for a heavier one), cutoff 2–3/10, envelope 5/10, ADSR about 0/4/3/4, chorus I. These are "plucked basses… for chugging lines" [50].
- **The Moroder bass**: an 8-step 8th-note sequence plus a delayed copy offset by a 16th, panned opposite (§2.1) [4][8]. The Hi-NRG version adds **octave alternation** [9].
- **Electro and Italo**: a 303 or monosynth line that changes pattern per section [12]. The Trans-X "Living On Video" "laser gun" bass is an SH-101 saw with sub at 32′, resonance high and a decaying VCA. **Halfway through a 4-bar loop the cutoff snaps from fully open to fully closed**, turning the buzz into a resonant "whistle". This is a ready-made surprise move [51].
- **"80s bass" patch**: three detuned saws at −2 oct (+9 cents), −2 oct (−4 cents) and −1 oct (+6 cents) with 7-voice unison, a 4-pole LP filter with an envelope (decay about 43%, sustain about 37%), slight attack to soften the transient, and chorus with a low cut [52].
- **Modulation**: detune oscillators "a few cents" for beating, push resonance high for squelch, and use a filter-envelope sweep so the bass cuts through [2].

### 4.5 Synths and sound design

- **Arps.** "Nightcall" is built on an 8-note arpeggio over the chords [38]. The patch: Juno-style saw with no sub, HPF on, cutoff about mid, resonance low to moderate, a pluck envelope with slight attack, chorus I. It has two quieter layers: a 2-note pattern with chorus II, and a dry mono doubling [38]. "A Real Hero" has an offbeat arp (F–B♭–F–A) using a soft-attack unison saw (cutoff very low, attack 68 ms, decay 1.2 s, sustain high) with chorus, 25% delay and 50% reverb, compressed after the effects [39].
- **Strings and pads.** The "Nightcall" strings are saw plus thin pulse (PW 85%), osc B +12 cents, filter 280 Hz, filter attack 44 ms and decay 5.4 s, with Juno-style chorus [38]. Timecop1983 strings: sub 1/10, cutoff 4, envelope 2.5, ADSR 1/6/8/6, chorus II [50]. ModeAudio adds detuned, "warbling" pads with LFO-filtered noise underneath [2].
- **Leads.** Bright square or saw, vibrato, "plenty of reverb", monophonic, "a few well-chosen notes" [2]. Desire's "Under Your Spell" lead is three detuned saws (−13 and +10 cents) with 4.56 Hz vibrato, drive, and doubled in octaves on scale runs [39]. Timecop1983 uses PWM leads (LFO PWM, ADSR 2/8/5/6) and long-release leads that "bleed into each other" [50].
- **Brass.** Prophet-5 or OB-X brass stabs (Robie's Prophet-5 on "Planet Rock" [14]; OB-X emulations in Perturbator [43]). Specific brass patch settings: unverified.
- **FM.** DX7 electric piano and bells (§2.4) [30]. Hammer liked the DX7 for "the way the upper harmonics pop up" with velocity [34]. In the engine, tie the FM modulator index to velocity.
- **Solos.** Sax ("wailing sax solos" with The Midnight [48]; Dick Morrissey's sax on *Blade Runner* "Love Theme" [32]) and a guitar-like distorted synth lead with pitch-bend, after Hammer [34]. Perturbator's later work uses real guitars [44].
- **Mix.** Tim McEwan: "I'm a chorus guy – I put chorus on everything and I don't use it sparingly". He uses Juno chorus emulation, tape saturation on drums, bass, synths and guitars, and Lexicon 224/480L and AMS RMX16 reverbs (480L on vocals). The Juno-106 is "all over" *Heroes*, and the Oberheim Matrix-6 is "the most synthwavey synth I've ever heard. You can crank the resonance" [47]. Timecop1983: heavy compression, wide analog-style delay, hall reverb **over 3 s** [50]. "A Real Hero" is about 35 cents flat of concert pitch, probably repitched [39]. Kavinsky's mix is "lightly distorted" with slight bit reduction [38]. Rosso Corsa: "highly-compressed dynamic ranges" [41]. Electro reference: the 808 drum bus is driven and glued at about 4 dB of compression [17].

Patterns:

1. About three-quarters of this sample is minor or Dorian. The tonic minor chord is the most-used chord in most entries [23]. ModeAudio: minor keys give "a brooding, soundtrack feel", major keys lean to "cheesier synth-pop" [2].
2. **The ♭VI–♭VII (VI–VII) motion is the genre's cadence.** It appears in Nightcall, Love on a Real Train, Axel F, Megamagic, Venger and the Italo I–♭VII–♭VI–♭VII. It resolves upward to i (or to I in the major-mode borrowing).
3. **Dorian colour.** A major IV in a minor key appears in Turbo Killer, Inferno Galore, You Spin Me Round and Blinding Lights.
4. **Extended and sus voicings** are a Mitch Murder trait: add9, 11, sus2, m7 [23].
5. **Slash chords and inversions**: G/B in Nightcall "has more sense of urgency" [38].
6. **Harmonic rhythm**: one chord per bar over 4-bar loops is standard [38][39]. ModeAudio also mentions "four chords per bar across four-bar patterns" [2].
7. **Major-key nostalgia**: Timecop1983's IV–V–vi–I (starting off the tonic, with a rising bass) [50] and Mixolydian loops [23]. The Midnight's keys could not be verified from an analytic source. The one Hooktheory entry ("Deep Blue") is B minor [23], so "The Midnight = major" is (unverified).
8. The i–VI–III–VII order was **not directly attested** in this sample. Its pieces (i–III–VII, VI–VII–i, III–VII) are attested many times.

### 4.7 Melody

- Leads are monophonic, sparse ("a few well-chosen notes"), bright and reverberant [2].
- Nightcall's melody range is G3–C5 [23]. Turbo Killer's melody spans F4–A♭5 (15 semitones), about 1.39 beats per note, 99% diatonic, mostly stepwise and "repeaty" [23].
- Arps outline the chord as 8 notes per bar (8ths) [38] or sit on the offbeats [39]. A second, sparser arp layer (2-note pattern) adds depth [38].
- Vocals: vocoded verses with sung choruses (Kavinsky [38]), dispassionate female vocals and vocoder (Trans-X [51]), spoken vocals (Perturbator 2021 [44]). Italo uses accented English [10].

### 4.8 Arrangement and energy

- **Two templates.**
  - *Pop song*: verse/chorus with 4-bar verses, 8-bar choruses and a middle eight that can modulate, as in Robotnick [12] and The Midnight and Kavinsky vocal tracks.
  - *Instrumental DJ/soundtrack*: elements enter and leave every 4 or 8 bars, with FX sweeps at the joins [11]. Timecop1983 starts from "a short loop — 6-7 seconds — that has all the sounds that are played in the middle of the track, and from there I start working on the build-up and break-down" [50]. That is a peak-first loop, subtracted into an intro and a breakdown.
- **Length.**
  - Synthwave singles run **3:29–6:29**: Turbo Killer 3:29, Roller Mobster 3:34, Nightcall 4:17, A Real Hero 4:28, Running in the Night 4:30, Overdrive 4:31, Future Club 4:49, Sunset 5:27, Los Angeles 6:29 [24].
  - Italo and electro 12-inch versions run **3:56–8:56**: Dolce Vita 3:56, Clear 4:55, Planet Rock 6:24–7:35, I Feel Love 8:14, I Like Chopin 8:56 [24].
- **Energy arc.** Build from kick and snare alone, hats later [2]. Use snare/tom fills at the end of 4- or 8-bar blocks [2][12]. Drop to 2 bars of drums with a reversed riser before the last chorus [12]. A siren or rising synth in the break raises tension [12]. Change the bass timbre after a chorus (the laser-gun filter snap) [51]. A solo section (sax, guitar-like synth or coronet-like synth) sits after the second chorus, sometimes in a new key [12][34][48]. The outro is the hook bass plus a reverb-drenched lead, faded [12].
- **Electro arrangement.** Within the rigid machine groove, a busier cowbell or percussion part is "best saved for choruses or breakdowns" [16]. "Planet Rock" uses an orchestra-hit and explosion break [14].

## Rules and parameters

### Tempo & feel
- Pick a substyle tempo: dreamwave/night-drive 86–116 BPM, Italo 103–122, electro 120–135, outrun/darksynth 128–147. [2][3][12][18][23][24]
- When tempo is under 100 BPM, allow a half-time feel (backbeat snare on step 13 only, or snare on 9 at half time) with probability 0.3. [2][23]
- Use straight 16ths (swing 50%) for synthwave and classic electro; allow 50–53% for Linn-disco beats and 55–65% only for the lo-fi electro variant. [3][17][18][19][28]
- Quantise drums hard. Velocity: electro uses 2 levels only (normal and accent); synthwave hats vary by ±10–15%; kick and snare have little or no variation. [2][3][16]
- Detune the whole track by 0–35 cents from A440 with probability 0.1 (the *Drive* "flat tape" feel). [39]
- Electro tempo drift: allow ±0.5 BPM slow wander to imitate an 808 master clock (Robotnick averaged 120.7). [12]

### Drums
- Synthwave (95–115): kick 1, 9; snare 5, 13; closed hat on all 16 steps; open hat on 14 (one per bar); in bar 2 add kick 16, claps 10, 11, 14, 15 and one 32nd hat before 13. [3]
- Outrun (128–140) and Italo: kick 1, 5, 9, 13; clap/snare 5, 13; closed hat on 3, 7, 11, 15 (Italo) or all 16ths (Robotnick style); open hat on 3, 7, 11, 15 or on the last offbeat of a 2-bar loop; optional ghost kick at step 15 (velocity −20%). [11][12][53]
- Dreamwave/Timecop mode: no hats or cymbals with probability 0.4; kick and snare only, toms for fills. [50]
- Electro bar A: kick 1, 4, 7, 11; bar B: kick 1, 7, 11; snare + clap 5, 13; open hat 5, 13; closed hat 1–4 and 9–12. [17]
- Miami-bass electro: kick bar 1 = 1, 7; bar 2 = 1, 7, 11, 14; snare + clap + rim 5, 13; closed hats on all 16ths except 2, 6, 10, 14; open hat 2 (bar 1) and 13 (bar 2); zap/perc on 8ths outside hat gaps. [18]
- Electro phrase end: double the last snare with a clap, put an open hat on step 16 of the last bar, and add a syncopated 808 cowbell only in choruses or breakdowns. [16]
- Gated reverb on snare, clap and toms: 100% wet send, pre-delay 0 ms, gate hold 300–500 ms, release under 50 ms; compress before the reverb; hats get a shorter hold (under 150 ms) or none. [25][26]
- Put a little gated/nonlinear reverb on the kick too (wet 5–15%) in synthwave. [3]
- Fills: snare 16ths on steps 13–16 or tom runs (high to low) on steps 9–16 every 4th or 8th bar. [2][12]
- Kits: 808 for electro, Linn/LM-1 for Italo and outrun, DrumTraks-style short digital samples for synthwave. Tune the snare to a related pitch of the kick (for example 2 octaves up). [3][17][19][27][28]

### Bass
- Default synthwave bass: root repeated in 8ths (steps 1, 3, 5, …, 15), plucked: saw (+ square or sub), cutoff about 150–250 Hz, envelope amount high, filter decay 400–500 ms, sustain 0, amp decay about 650 ms, sustain about 0.3, mono unison with 5–9 cents detune. [2][39][50]
- Moroder/Hi-NRG bass: 8-step 8th-note sequence alternating root and octave, plus a delayed copy offset by one 16th and panned opposite (L dry / R echo). [4][8][9]
- Nightcall-style chorus bass: two saws an octave apart, LPF about 1.1 kHz, drive on; optionally double with a piano in octaves. [38]
- Resonant sweep bass/lead: saws an octave apart, resonance at 60–80% of maximum, cutoff about 700 Hz with a 2.5 s downward envelope sweep, delayed vibrato and filter LFO at about 5.3 Hz faded in over about 1 s, plus a sub layer low-passed at about 220 Hz. [38]
- 80s bass stack: saws at −2 oct (+9 cents), −2 oct (−4 cents) and −1 oct (+6 cents), 4-pole LPF, filter decay about 40%, sustain about 35%, attack of a few ms, chorus with low cut. [52]
- Electro and Italo bass: a 303- or SH-101-style mono line, one pattern per section (a new pattern for the bridge, return in the chorus). [12][51]
- Detune bass oscillators by 3–10 cents for beating; raise resonance for squelch in electro and Italo. [2]

### Harmony
- Choose minor/Aeolian 60%, Dorian 15%, Phrygian 5% (electro), major 15%, Mixolydian 5%. [2][23]
- Loop 4 chords over 4 bars, one chord per bar; allow 2-chord vamps (i–v) for Italo and electro verses. [23][38]
- Minor progression pool (weight the VI–VII ascent highly): i–VII–VI–iv (with VII in first inversion), VI–VII–v–VI, VI–VII–i, i–VI–VII–i, i–III–VII, i–III–v–IV, III–iv–VI–i–VII, iv–i–III–V. [23][38]
- Dorian option: make IV major in a minor key (i–III–IV, i–v–VII–IV). [23]
- Major pool: IV–V–vi–I, I–♭VII–♭VI–♭VII, I–♭III–♭VII–♭VI–IV (Mixolydian), I–V–vi–IV–V (Italo). [11][23][50]
- Voicings: add9, 11, sus2, m7 on 30–50% of chords in dreamwave/Mitch Murder mode; triads in darksynth. [23]
- Use slash chords: put the VII chord over its 3rd (G/B in A minor) with probability 0.3. [38]
- Darksynth: allow a harmonic-minor major V and a ♭II chord. [23]

### Melody/hooks
- Make the arp the hook: 8 notes per bar (8ths) spelling the current chord, or offbeat-only 8ths (steps 3, 7, 11, 15). [38][39]
- Add a second, sparser arp layer (2-note pattern, −10 dB) and a dry mono doubling. [38]
- Keep the lead monophonic, mostly stepwise, at least 95% diatonic, about 1.4 beats per note, range 12–15 semitones (for example F4–A♭5). [2][23]
- Double scale runs in octaves. [39]
- In the solo section, use a sax-like or guitar-like lead with pitch-bend and vibrato; Hammer style: distorted lead with bends of ±2 semitones (bend depth unverified). [34][39][48]
- Vocal-style hooks: vocoder on verses, clean chorus hook. [38][51]

### Sound design
- Juno-style poly as the default voice: saw (+PWM), sub 0–10, 24 dB LPF, HPF for layers, chorus I (0.4 Hz) or II (0.6 Hz) or both, always on for pads, arps and leads. [29][38][50]
- Pads/strings: saw + pulse (PW about 85%), +12 cents detune, LPF about 280 Hz, filter attack about 44 ms, decay about 5 s; or Juno strings ADSR (0.1, 0.6, 0.8, 0.6 of range). [38][50]
- Leads: saw or square, vibrato at 4.5–5.5 Hz with depth of a few cents (delayed onset), reverb 30–50% wet, delay about 25%. [2][38][39]
- FM (4-op): an electric piano with velocity mapped to modulation index, bell patches, and a DX-style plucked bass. [30][34]
- Brass stabs: a polysynth unison saw with a filter envelope (Prophet/OB style) on chord hits. [14][43][47]
- Reverbs: hall at 3 s or more for dreamwave, gated/nonlinear for drums. Compress after delay and reverb to "squash the tails". [25][39][50]
- Put chorus on most buses (bass with a low cut, guitar-like leads, pads); add tape saturation on drums, bass and synths. [47][52]
- Master: hard bus compression and light saturation or bit reduction for darksynth and Kavinsky grit. [38][41]
- Electro FX: vocoder or robotic speech, orchestra hits, explosion/noise sweeps, sonar pings (Drexciya). [13][14][21]

### Arrangement & energy
- Pop template (vocal synthwave and Italo): intro 4–8 bars, verse 4 or 8, chorus 8, verse, chorus, middle eight 8–12, solo 4–8, drum break 2 + riser, chorus ×2, outro fade. Target 3:30–6:30. [12][24]
- DJ/instrumental template: start drums only (kick + snare), add hats at bar 9, bass at bar 17; add or remove one element every 4 or 8 bars; FX sweep at each 8- or 16-bar boundary. Target 4:30–9:00 for electro and Italo 12-inch versions. [2][11][24]
- Design the peak first (a 6–7 s loop with all parts) and derive the intro and breakdown by subtraction. [50]
- End every 8-bar block with a snare or tom fill; hold back the busiest percussion (cowbell, congas) for choruses and breakdowns. [2][12][16][53]
- Breakdown: 2 bars of drums only, then a reversed reverb riser into the final chorus. [12]

### Variation/surprise
- Key change in the bridge: +3 semitones (minor to minor a third up), then return; or a solo moved to the major key a 5th above the original tonic, with probability 0.15. [12]
- Bass-timbre snap: cut the filter from open to closed at the midpoint of a 4-bar phrase after a chorus ("laser gun"). [51]
- A delayed vibrato or filter-LFO swell on long notes (fade in over about 1 s). [38]
- A siren synth (pitch rising and falling over 2–4 bars) in breaks and the outro. [12]
- A rogue 32nd-note hat or an extra kick on step 16 at a turnaround. [3]
- Electro: alternate kick bars (1, 4, 7, 11 with 1, 7, 11) and move a hat gap each 4 bars. [17][18]
- Start the track off the downbeat (an open hat before bar 1) with probability 0.1. [12]
- A whole-mix pitch offset (up to −35 cents) or slight bit reduction as a "tape/VHS" character variant. [38][39]

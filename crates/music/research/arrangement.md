# Arrangement and layering in electronic dance music

Research for the per-instrument-timeline arranger. Scope: how EDM tracks are put together over time from looped layers (any genre); intros and outros for DJs; breakdown → build → drop; phrase-end fills; genre differences; energy over a track and over a set. Each genre's sound is covered in another file. Bar counts assume 4/4. "Layer" means one instrument's loop (kick, hats, bass, pad, …). For time conversions: one bar = 240/BPM seconds, so 16 bars ≈ 30 s at 128 BPM, ≈ 27.8 s at 138 BPM, ≈ 22 s at 174 BPM.

Most numbers below come from (a) academic work (Garcia, Solberg, Yadati et al., Osborn, Smith, Kim et al., Vande Veire & De Bie) and (b) bar-numbered track breakdowns in Attack Magazine's "Deconstructed" series. I counted bars and layers in (b) myself where the article lists entries; such counts say "(my count)".

## 1. The phrase grid: loops change on 4/8/16/32-bar boundaries

**The model.** Garcia describes EDM loops as "riffs of modular length that one strongly expects to repeat". Looping, in his words, is "the practice of layering, adding and subtracting loops" [1 ¶3.9]. Structural changes fall on multiples of four, "whether four beats, four bars, eight bars or more" [1 ¶4.3]. Solberg says the basic unit is "often consisting of 2 or 4 bars". Elements "are most often introduced, changed and removed after 2, 4, 8, 16 or 32 bars", so the groove "expands in textural density rather than length" [2 p.66]. Future Music gives a working rule of thumb: dance tunes "generally progress in 16-bar sections". Within those sections, elements are 1-, 2-, 4- or 8-bar repeating phrases, and they "tend to be changed (added, removed or altered) every four or eight bars" [9].

**Measured evidence.**
- *Drum and bass:* Vande Veire & De Bie built an automatic DJ on the assumption that "phrases are a multiple of 8 measures long" and that segments are a whole number of phrases. Their 160 annotated songs bore this out. The assumption failed for only 4 songs, and 3 more had the wrong 8-bar offset; 95.3% of songs got structurally correct annotations [8 §3, §5.4].
- *DJ mixes, mostly house and trance, average 127 BPM:* in 1,557 real DJ mixes (20,765 transitions), the histogram of transition lengths peaks every 32 beats (8 bars), "which is often considered as a phrase" [7 §6.3]. Cue-out points also cluster every 32 beats [7].
- *Cue agreement between DJs on the same track:* 23.6% of cue pairs are identical, 40.4% fall within 4 beats, 73.6% within "8 measures" and 86.2% within "16 measures". The paper's unit for the last two figures is ambiguous [7 §6.4].

**Deliberate exceptions exist and work as surprise:**
- Reese's "Just Want Another Chance" silences the kick after 14 bars, which the article calls an unusual non-multiple-of-four turnaround [15].
- Special Request cuts break and bass at bar 112, one bar before the expected turnaround at 113 [20].
- A 7-bar spoken-word break in "Hackney Parrot" shifts the whole arrangement by one bar [19].
- Computer Music suggests inserting an extra 1-, 2- or 4-bar gap before major transitions. It warns to keep such pauses away from the very start and end, "as they'll knock the DJ's intro or outro out of place" [10].

**Additive vs subtractive.**
- *Additive* building means stacking loops one at a time. Garcia's Plastikman analysis is an "accumulative" build: a 4-bar flute fade-in, then congas, then a sudden three-part drum loop. Just before the peak, the triangle loop is removed "four bars beforehand and the drum loop one bar beforehand" [1 ¶4.3].
- *Subtractive* arrangement means copying every part across the whole timeline, then muting parts to carve out the intro and the sections [10].
- Detroit-school tracks are arranged almost entirely by muting and unmuting loops:
  - Jeff Mills' "The Bells" has "no programmed drum fills"; every transition is a mute or unmute on the mixer [14].
  - Reese uses no fills and builds through "drops and reintroductions" [15].
  - Âme's "Rej" also "avoids fills or percussion dropouts" and uses synths to mark changes [17].

Patterns. All counts below are my own, from the table.
- **Kick first.** The kick sounds in bar 1 in 5 of the 6 four-on-the-floor tracks. Attack notes that "The Bells" starts "as so many other techno tracks do, with a kick drum" [14].
- **Hats and rides come next**, at bar 9 in Mills and Lone, so the second layer enters after 8 bars.
- **Bass is the most variable layer.** It enters at bar 1 (Âme), bar 9 (Reese), bar 17 (jungle), bar 41 (Disclosure), bar 65 (Mills), at the drop (Lone), or after 3 minutes (Tiga). It is held back from the first 8 bars in 6 of 7 tracks.
- **Lead or hook material** either stays out of the intro entirely or appears as a bare motif over the kick (Mills, Tiga). Future Music advises: "Avoid overly melodic elements at the beginning or end of a track" [9].
- **Density** (my count of simultaneous layers):
  - Mills: 2 → 4 (bar 9) → 5 (bar 17), then 1 at bar 25.
  - Lone: 2 → 3 → 5–6 before the bar-33 breakdown.
  - Reese: 2 for bars 1–8, then ≈8 from bar 9.
  - Âme: 4 → 6 → ≈9–10 by bar 33.
  - Peak sections run about 8–12 layers. Breakdowns fall to 1–3.
- **Rate of change.** In every deconstruction, a layer enters, exits or changes at least every 16 bars, and usually every 8 [9][14–20]. Jungle and DnB keep the same 16-bar grid [19][20]. Tessela adds a 4-bar sub-phrase in which a second break plays in bars 2 and 4 of each 4-bar phrase [19].

## 3. DJ-friendly intros, outros, key and edits

**Purpose.** DJs beatmatch the incoming track while the outgoing one plays. Transitions therefore overlap the incoming track's intro with the outgoing track's outro or main section. The DJ also uses EQ: when the kicks line up, the lows of one track are cut while the other's are boosted [Wikipedia: Beatmatching, via 36]. To keep the phrase structure intact, it "is often enough to start the track to be mixed in at a phrase boundary" [36].

**Lengths.**
- Lost Stories Academy: DJs need "16 or 32 bars of simple elements at the beginning". Typical contents are kick, hats or percussion, minimal bass and light FX, with no full melody or vocal [33].
- Deconstructed intros:
  - 8 bars: Reese kick+clap [15]; Mills and Lone kick-led [14][16].
  - 16 bars: Tiga, Disclosure, Special Request [18][13][20].
  - 24 bars: Tessela [19].
  - 48 bars of building groove before the first riser: Âme [17].
- Psytrance intros are sparse and meant to blend into the previous track's outro. The outro strips back until "only kick and bass remain" [21].
- Outros in the deconstructions:
  - Âme ends on kick plus two shakers for the last 16 bars [17].
  - Lone drops kick and bass and leaves percussion [16].
  - In Disclosure's last 8 bars the hats quit and come back, with a low-pass filter on the kick over the final 4 [13].
  - Lone marks the start of the ending with a momentary beat drop at bar 168 [16].

**How long a transition lasts.**
- Kim et al.'s transition lengths cluster at multiples of 8 bars [7].
- Vande Veire & De Bie's DnB auto-DJ uses fades of 16 bars, plus one 32-bar fade-out, with an EQ "bass swap": during the fade-in the incoming track's bass and treble stay "almost entirely filtered out". At the switch point they jump to full while the outgoing track's are cut, so only one track's bass is heard at any moment [8 §4.3].
- The three transition types, with cue points relative to structure [8 §4.2.3]:
  - **Double drop:** both tracks cued 16 bars before a drop and their drops aligned; 16-bar fade-in, 32-bar fade-out.
  - **Rolling:** outgoing track cued 32 bars before its high→low boundary, incoming track 16 bars before its drop; 16/16 fades.
  - **Relaxed:** outgoing track cued 16 bars before high→low; incoming track starts from the top; 16/16 fades.
  - These types follow a Markov chain (rows = previous type):
    - after relaxed: 0 / 0.7 / 0.3 (relaxed / rolling / double drop);
    - after rolling: 0.2 / 0.8 / 0;
    - after double drop: 0.2 / 0.8 / 0.
  - Double drops are never chained [8 Table 3].

**Key.** Camelot rules from Mixed In Key [30]:
- stay on the same code (5A→5A);
- move ±1 number (8A→9A, 8A→7A);
- switch letter at the same number (8A→8B, relative major/minor).

An "energy boost" is +2 numbers (5A→7A), used "every 20-30 minutes, but not ALL the time". A −5 variant (8A→3A) is attributed to Armin van Buuren [31]. In practice DJs rarely re-pitch: only 2.5% of 24,202 played tracks were transposed, 94.3% of those by one semitone. 86.1% of tempo adjustments are under 5% [7 §6.1–6.2].

**Extended vs radio edit.**
- Radio edits run "between three and five minutes". They cut intros and outros, remove buildups and fade out earlier, sometimes removing nearly half the song [28].
- Extended or club versions descend from disco 12-inches. Tom Moulton stretched sub-3-minute songs to about double length and is credited with "the disco break". Examples: "Ten Percent" ran 9:43 against a 3:05 7-inch edit [35][27].
- Psytrance tracks commonly run 7–9 minutes [21].
- In Kim et al.'s mixes, unique tracks average 4.0 minutes, with 15.5 tracks per 60.5-minute mix [7].

## 4. Breakdown → build → drop

**Definitions.**
- **Breakdown:** the texture becomes "considerably thinner or even … entirely changed". Several layers are removed, "most importantly the bass and the bass drum" [2 p.67]. Smith defines breakdowns by "thin texture and relatively quiet volume", usually mid-track [5].
- **Build:** "the different instrument layers are built up one after one … the rhythmic structures being constantly compressed" [2 p.67].
- **Drop:** a drop "occurs after a build … and is followed by the re-introduction of the full bassline" [3 §1, citing Butler]. Smith reserves "drop" for the moment the low frequencies and kick return. He calls the following full section the *core* [5].
- Osborn's "riser" section has rising pitch, usually a filter sweep on one sample. It "nearly always" contains a *build*, "a rhythmic crescendo, usually on a synthesized snare drum, in which attack onsets become exponentially faster, moving from quarter notes, to eighth notes, to sixteenth notes, typically ending with a blurred roll". It also has "the notable absence of a four-on-the-floor kick" [6 pp.37–38].

**What drops out.**
- Kick and bass first [2][9]. Future Music also suggests dropping hats and shakers, which "are best saved for when it's really 'pumping'", and lowering the whole breakdown by "a few dB" [9].
- Some breakdowns keep a skeleton:
  - Kronos keeps only the clap on 2 and 4 at first, adding a soft off-beat hat later [12].
  - Âme keeps "kick and bass, the primal elements" [17].
  - Model 500 starts its 16-bar breakdown with kick, snare and bass [Attack summary, via search; not fetched].
- The bass may keep playing with its lows cut [12].
- In trance, breakdowns remove beats and percussion entirely, leaving melody or atmosphere alone [22].

**Lengths observed.**

| Track | Breakdown | Build | Notes |

Big-room house typically has two build-ups and two drops, one build much longer than the other [25]. Butler's and Snoman's prototypes, as summarised by Solberg, also have two build/drop pairs, "where the second pair … is even more intense than the first" [2 p.67].

**Build devices.**
- *Uplifters / risers.* An uplifter is "a sound being gradually pitched further and further in an upward direction". Usually it is several long synth sounds pitched up plus "the typical white noise uplifter" [2 p.70]. Osborn's riser keeps the fundamental and cuts lows progressively, so the timbre brightens [6].
- *Drum-roll effect.* The roll subdivides from quarter notes to a roll; in "Body" it fills the last 4 bars and gets louder as it goes. Because dancers need a steady tempo, subdividing gives "the illusion … of a tempo increase" [2 p.70].
  - Attack's ten roll recipes [11]:
    - a straight 16th roll of one bar or longer;
    - a gain or velocity ramp (Velocity Out Hi automated 1→127, with a little randomisation);
    - a filter rising to fully open at the drop, on a parabolic curve;
    - a pitch rise across the bar;
    - accent on the first of each 4 sixteenths;
    - a "speed-up" of 4 quarters → 4 eighths → 8 sixteenths;
    - a roll that stops early at bar position 2.4, with the pause filled by a jarring sound;
    - a 16th roll layered with a quarter-note snare.
  - "Good Life" uses 32nd-note fills in the roll. Its kick and vocal chop go from quarters to eighths in the last 4 bars [12].
- *Filter sweeps.* Future Music: removing "the entire bottom end with a high-pass filter … just before a new section works a treat". "Opening up a low-pass filter during a breakdown can put the crowd into a frenzy" [9]. Osborn's riser is an automated high-pass [6]. In Kronos, pads start with highs cut and open over the 32 bars [12].
- *Pitch and register.* In "Body", build-up 2 drops the bass and kick entirely and pitches the rest upward, so the spectrum focuses high before the full-range return [2 p.71].

**The gap before the drop.**
- Future Music: when coming out of a breakdown, mute everything except one snare hit on the downbeat and leave the reverb tails ringing [9].
- Some builds end with "a bar of silence" (Osborn 2019, via [38]). Wikipedia describes "a beat of silence" before the drop [27].
- In the deconstructions:
  - "Good Life" cuts everything but the piano two beats before the end of bar 24 [12].
  - Lone cuts the build one bar early, adds a vocal sample, then a single kick "feint" bar before the drop [16].
  - Psytrance pauses for about 4 beats [21].
  - Madeon's "micro vacuum": a bass slide plus kick alone on beat 1, with the rest of the groove withheld until beat 2 [2 pp.74–76].
- The intensity is greatest when listeners are "dropped back down into the anticipated … steady framework" [2].

**Impacts, downlifters and sidechain.**
- A riser often ends in a faller, the white-noise hiss that also starts the drop [12]. Other examples:
  - Âme pairs each white-noise riser with a splash cymbal on the next downbeat [17].
  - Kronos enters its breakdown on a riser and a metallic hit [12].
  - Disclosure's build mixes rising and falling FX with a widening vocal reverb send [13].
- At Madeon's drop, the bass is "sidechained to the bass drum to create a pumping effect" [2 p.74]. Side-chaining basslines to the kick is standard EDM practice [29].
- Mixed In Key's Energy Level 7 ("peak time") is defined by "pumping percussion and bass sidechain" [32].

## 5. Fills and transitions at phrase ends

Phrase-end markers in the deconstructions:
- **Kick drop-outs.**
  - Reese drops the kick "before the turnaround" (bars ~8, 41, 87, 145), "letting us (and the DJ) know that a change is coming" [15].
  - Mills: a 2-bar kick+clap drop around bars 49–51, and a 1-bar break of hats, rides and claps at ~64 [14].
  - Lone: a momentary beat drop at 168 [16].
- **Snare and percussion fills.**
  - Reese adds an extra snare hit at the end of each 4-bar block [15].
  - Special Request uses filter-and-flange fills at bars 24 and 32, plus Amen snare climbs at bars 39 and 47, i.e. bar 7 of each 8 [20].
  - Tessela closes each turnaround with a stick fill, and a dub siren sounds before each turnaround in the intro [19].
  - Âme uses an off-time roll at each 16-bar loop end [17].
  - Lone uses a break-pattern change at the end of a 16-bar section as a "mini fill" [16].
- **Reverses and sweeps.** Tiga: a full-mix reverse at bar 121 into bar 129 [18]. Future Music lists "rising or falling sound FX, reverse cymbals and automated delay or reverb" as the anticipation devices [9].
- **Anacrusis entries.** In Tiga, the bar-17 synth and bar-33 vocal both start just before the downbeat [18].
- **Bass drop-outs.** Tessela mutes the bass for one bar, and the bass returns one bar before the turnaround [19].
- **Variety.** Don't use the same fill at every boundary. Save the strongest signal for the biggest change [37].

More detail:
- **Progressive house:** builds of "up to four minutes". Phrases are 4, 8 or 16 bars, each often starting with a new melody or rhythm. The style deliberately lacks "anthemic choruses, crescendos, and drum rolls" [26].
- **Trance:** before the breakdown, "the lead motif is often introduced in a sliced and simplified form" [22, citing Snoman 2009].
- **Pop-EDM rotations:** verse–riserchorus–drop and the most common verse–prechorus–riserchorus–drop. Drops after 2016 often add effected vocal samples, where 2011–14 drops were instrumental [6].
- **DnB tempo:** 160–190 BPM assumed in [8]. Wikipedia gives 165–185 and 160–180 [24].

## 7. Energy curves

**Within a track.**
- Solberg draws an intensity curve that follows the amplitude graph: flat but slowly rising intros, a dip at the breakdown, and a peak at the drop [2 Fig.2].
- Motion capture of dancers confirms this: "the quantity of the dancers' movement reduced during the breakdown, and increased at the drop" [34].
- Osborn: drops "present the highest statistical value in several measurable parameters, especially loudest volume and thickest texture" [6].
- Yadati et al. found the build "can be clearly observed in the spectrogram". Spectrogram statistics plus MFCCs detect drops with F1 = 0.71 at a 15 s tolerance, from SoundCloud-comment labels on 100 tracks [3].
- Future Music: "the first three quarters of a track generally consists of 'build-ups' (with intermittent breaks)". It gives a "quick logically laid-out" map: main breakdown at bar 129, mini breakdown at bar 65, drop to outro beats at bar 193, i.e. a ~256-bar track [9].

**Over a set.**
- Butler, as summarised in [8]: the DJ starts relaxed, builds energy through increasingly energetic songs, plays calmer songs after the climax, then builds toward a second climax.
- Mixed In Key's 1–10 scale [32]:
  - start a club set at level 5, move to 6 as people arrive, then 7;
  - save 8–9 for the headliner or climax;
  - move one step at a time ("skipping from Energy Level 5 > 7" is "too abrupt");
  - brief dips are fine (e.g. 6 → 4 → 6);
  - famous sets average 6.2–7.5.
- Vande Veire's Markov chain mixes flow (rolling), rest (relaxed) and climax (double drop) [8].

**Gaps.** I could not access Butler's *Unlocking the Groove* (2006) or Snoman's *Dance Music Manual* directly. They are cited here through Solberg [2], Smith [5], Vande Veire [8] and Wikipedia [22]. I found no work by Brøvig-Hanssen specifically on EDM form, and no Spotify or Beatport corpus statistics on section lengths. Trance and dubstep bar counts come from tutorials, Wikipedia and synthesis, not measured corpora.

## Rules and parameters

### Tempo & feel
- Hold tempo constant for the whole track. Builds fake acceleration by subdividing the snare roll, not by changing BPM. [2][11]
- Express all arrangement lengths in bars at the genre's tempo. For logging, a 16-bar section lasts 3840/BPM seconds (30 s at 128, 22 s at 174). [7]

### Drums
- Kick enters at bar 1 in four-on-the-floor genres (house, techno, trance). [14][16][18]
- In DnB and jungle, start the intro with a processed or filtered break. [20][19]
- Add the second drum layer (off-beat hat or ride) at bar 9. Add the clap or snare by bar 17. [14][16]
- At a breakdown, remove the kick (and hats and shakers) first. Optionally keep the clap on 2 and 4 (steps 5, 13). [9][12]
- Snare roll: last 4–8 bars of the build, in two to four stages:
  - quarters (steps 1,5,9,13);
  - 8ths (every odd step);
  - 16ths (every step);
  - optionally 32nds in the final bar.
  - Velocity ramps 1→127; accent the first of each 4 sixteenths. [2][6][11][12]
- In 30% of builds, stop the roll early at beat 2.4 or 3 of the last bar and leave silence. [11][12]
- Before a phrase turnaround, mute the kick for the last 1–2 bars, or for 2 beats. Do this at most once per 16 bars. [14][15]
- Techno profile: no fills. Mark transitions only by muting or unmuting layers. [14][15][17]

### Bass
- Keep the bass out of the first 8 bars, except deep-house/techno "kick + bass" intros, which are allowed. [17]
- Pick the bass entry from {9, 17, 33, 41, 65} or "at first drop", weighted by genre: house 17–41; techno 9–65; DnB/dubstep 17 or first drop. [13][14][15][16][20]
- Remove the bass during the breakdown and build. Alternatively keep it with lows high-passed (HPF ≥ 150 Hz). [2][12]
- Bring the bass back exactly on the drop downbeat. Optionally lead with a 1-beat downward pitch slide on beat 1 and return the rest of the groove on beat 2. [2]
- Sidechain the bass and pads to the kick in drop and core sections, recovering within about a quarter note. Disable or reduce sidechain in breakdowns. [2][29][32]

### Harmony
- In breakdowns, change the chord loop every 8 bars: the full progression, then shortened, then one repeated chord in the last 8. [12]
- Keep pitched material (chords, lead) out of the first and last 16 bars of club versions. [9][33]
- For set or radio sequencing, chain keys by the Camelot rules: same code, ±1 number, or A↔B. Allow an energy-boost move of +2 at most every 20–30 minutes. [30][31]

### Melody / hooks
- Before the breakdown, present the lead as a sliced, simplified fragment. Give the full melody in the breakdown and at the final climax (trance). [22]
- Pop-EDM: put the title hook in the riserchorus (no kick, rising filter). Make the drop an instrumental or chopped-vocal hook. [6]

### Sound design
- Riser: white noise plus 1–3 synth layers with pitch or HPF sweeping upward over 4–16 bars, ending exactly at the drop downbeat. [2][6][12]
- Impact: crash, splash or "faller" (a falling white-noise hiss) on the drop downbeat, and on every section downbeat following a riser. [12][17]
- Filters:
  - build: HPF on the music bus, rising to remove the bottom end just before the drop;
  - breakdown: LPF on pads, opening over the section.
  - Reset both instantly at the drop. [9][12]
- Lower breakdown level by 2–4 dB relative to the core. [9]
- Pre-drop gap: 2 beats to 1 bar of silence, with reverb tails left ringing. Optionally a single snare hit on the last downbeat. [9][12][16][21]

### Arrangement & energy
- Every section length is a multiple of 8 bars. Every layer entry, exit or change falls on a multiple of 4 bars. At least one layer changes every 16 bars, typically every 8. [1][2][8][9]
- DJ intro: 16 or 32 bars of drums plus at most 1 other non-melodic layer. DJ outro: mirror it, removing layers down to kick + percussion over the last 16–32 bars. Never put a gap in the first or last 32 bars. [10][33][17][13]
- Entry order: kick → hats/ride → clap/snare → percussion → bass → pads/chords → lead/hook → FX ear-candy.
  - Each entry lands 8 or 16 bars after the previous one.
  - The bass and lead slots may move later; hats never come before the kick, except in house intros that start with clap and hat. [13][14][16][17]
- Layer count: intro 1–3; groove 4–6; core/drop 8–12; breakdown 1–3; outro 2–3. (My count from [14–17].)
- Peak pattern: intro → groove → main 1 → breakdown → build → drop/main 2 → outro, with the second build longer and the second drop denser than the first. [2][25]
- Energy curve:
  - rises stepwise across the first ~60% of the track;
  - dips at about 50–65% (main breakdown);
  - peaks at the drop that follows;
  - decays over the outro. [2][9][21]
- Breakdown lengths by genre:
  - house: 16–64 bars;
  - techno: 1–16 bars;
  - trance: 32–64 bars;
  - DnB/dubstep: 16–32 bars;
  - pop-EDM riserchorus: 8–16 bars. [5][12][13][14][21]
- Build lengths: 8 bars by default, 4–16 allowed; a psytrance build may be 32. [12][13][21]
- Set level: a radio or "playlist" mode should move energy by at most 1 step per track. It should open at 50–60% of maximum and save the top two levels for a climax. [32][8]
- DJ-style transitions between generated tracks:
  - overlap 16 bars, or 32 bars for a double drop;
  - bass swap at the midpoint.
  - Pick the type from the Markov table: relaxed→{rolling 0.7, double 0.3}; rolling→{relaxed 0.2, rolling 0.8}; double→{relaxed 0.2, rolling 0.8}. [8]

### Variation / surprise
- 10–20% of tracks: insert one extra 1-, 2- or 4-bar gap before a major transition, never in the first or last 32 bars. [10]
- 10%: fake-out. The build cuts 1 bar early; then either a single-kick bar or a filtered "false drop" of 8 bars, followed by the real drop. [16]
- 5–10%: an off-grid turnaround, ending a section 1 bar early (7, 15 or 31 bars) or after 14 bars. [15][19][20]
- Never use the same fill type at two consecutive phrase boundaries. Reserve the loudest fill for the bar before the drop. [37]
- Second drop: change at least one layer compared with the first (new bass pattern, drum loop, or added top layer). [23][13]
